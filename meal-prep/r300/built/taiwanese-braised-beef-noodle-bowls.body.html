      Makes 14 servings · ~628 cal · 38g protein · 74g carbs · 20g fat · ~$3.37 per serving.

      Taiwanese beef noodle is the closest thing Taiwan has to a national dish, and it is a serious bowl, not a thin soup with a few pieces of meat floating in it. Chuck roast braises in soy sauce and doubanjiang until it falls apart, and the broth that comes out of it is worth the afternoon by itself. Each bowl runs 628 calories and 38 grams of protein for about $3.37 a serving.

      Shop Smart

      • Doubanjiang, the fermented chili bean paste, is the flavor of this dish and there is no real substitute. It costs a few dollars for a jar that lasts through many batches, so the per-bowl cost of it is basically nothing.
      • Lo mein noodles by the pack are far cheaper than fresh noodles and hold up better in a container. Cook them a minute under the package time so they do not go mushy on reheat.
      • Chuck roast is the biggest line and the correct cut. This is a long braise, so a pricier cut would give you nothing back for the extra money.
      • Green onions and cilantro are the cheapest upgrade on the list. A bunch of each turns a plain bowl into something that looks like it came from a shop, and they cost less than a soda.

      Make It

      1. Weigh your empty mixing pot and write the number down for portioning later.
      2. Cut the chuck into large chunks and blanch it in boiling water for a few minutes, then drain and rinse. This pulls out the scum and gives you a clear broth instead of a cloudy one.
      3. Heat the vegetable oil in a heavy pot and fry the ginger, garlic, yellow onion, and the white parts of the green onions until fragrant.
      4. Stir in the doubanjiang, tomato paste, and dried arbol chiles and cook for a minute until the oil turns red.
      5. Add the beef back with the diced tomatoes, soy sauce, chicken broth, sugar, and enough water to cover. Bring it to a simmer and skim off anything that rises.
      6. Cover and simmer low for 2 to 3 hours, until the beef is tender enough to break with chopsticks. Taste the broth and adjust the salt at the end, not the beginning.
      7. Cook the lo mein noodles just short of done, drain, and rinse them in cold water so they stop cooking and do not clump.
      8. Divide the noodles into the containers, ladle the beef and broth over the top, and finish with the green onion tops and chopped cilantro.

      Portion It

      Weigh the noodles into the containers first so each one gets the same base, then put the pot on the scale, subtract the empty-pot weight, and divide the beef and broth by 14. Every bowl lands at 628 calories that way. If you are eating these across several days, keep the broth in a separate container and pour it over hot. Noodles sitting in liquid all week turn to paste.
