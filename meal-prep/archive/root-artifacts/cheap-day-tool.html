How this number is computed

The rules are fixed and public, so the number means the same thing every day. The computer must build a day that satisfies all of these:

  • About 2,000 calories and at least 50 grams of protein. A standard adult day, with enough protein to matter.
  • No single food over 400 calories. That is a fifth of the day. It keeps the answer looking like a plate of food instead of a bucket of one thing.
  • Cooking fat capped at 200 calories. About a tablespoon and a half of oil or butter. Enough to cook with. It is not treated as a food group.
  • At least 200 calories from fruit or vegetables.
  • At least 20 grams of protein from meat, eggs, or dairy. Grains and beans carry protein too, but complete protein has to be anchored somewhere.

Within those rules it works cheapest first: fruit or vegetable calories first, then the cheapest complete protein, then the cheapest remaining calories, and if protein still falls short it swaps in the cheapest protein until it is met. Calories and protein come from nutrition labels in our recipe database. Prices come from our weekly Omaha price check across Walmart, Baker's, Family Fare, Hy-Vee, Aldi, and Sam's Club, taking each item's cheapest store.

Read this before you quote the number

  • This is a floor, not a meal plan. It proves what is mathematically possible at today's prices. A real week of eating needs more variety than any single cheapest day.
  • Meat is priced at raw weight. Cooked portions weigh less.
  • Seasonings, cooking fuel, water, and equipment are not counted. The number assumes a working kitchen.
  • It uses unit prices across multiple stores. You cannot buy 4 tablespoons of peanut butter; you buy a jar. The per-day math is honest, but the checkout total on day one is higher, and getting the cheapest price on every item can mean more than one store.
  • Prices are this week's cheapest we found. Your store, your week, and your shelf may differ.
  • This is not medical or dietary advice. It is arithmetic about groceries.
