# Style Guide — "Thrifty Crew" Weekly Lessons

*(Read this fully before writing any lesson. Every lesson must obey it.)*

## What this product is
A very-low-cost ($5/mo) membership for **parents of teens (roughly 11–18)**. Each week delivers
one practical lesson that helps a parent teach their kid about **money** and **setting them up
for success in life**. The reader is the **parent**. The kid is who they're helping. Lessons are
the inspiration of a full-length book by a warm, been-there mentor — but these lessons are their
own thing, written directly to parents.

## The voice (this is the most important section)
- **Warm mentor, on the parent's side.** Encouraging, honest, calm. Never preachy, never
  lecturing, never shaming. The parent is doing their best; we're handing them tools.
- **Plain language.** Short, punchy sentences mixed with longer ones. Talk like a smart friend
  across the kitchen table, not a textbook or a financial advisor.
- **Direct address.** Speak to the parent as "you." Refer to their child as "your kid" / "your
  teen."
- **Concrete over abstract.** Real numbers, real scripts, real examples. Show, don't preach.
- **Honest about the hard parts.** Don't pretend this is easy or that the parent has done it
  perfectly. Acknowledge the friction (eye-rolls, "I know, Mom," awkward money talks).
- **No fluff, no filler.** Every paragraph earns its place.

## Hard rules (do NOT break these)
1. **NO addiction, alcohol, drugs, recovery, jail, DUIs, or AA.** The source book draws on the
   author's recovery story; we are deliberately leaving ALL of that out. Translate the *wisdom*,
   drop the backstory.
2. **Keep it universal and welcoming.** No religion, no politics, no preaching. Values-based, not
   faith-based — so it works for every family. (Where the book leaned on faith, replace with a
   universal value like character, contentment, or purpose.)
3. **Not financial advice.** When a lesson touches investing, include a light, friendly caveat
   ("This isn't financial advice — it's a starting point; check with a professional before
   investing real money"). Keep examples illustrative.
4. **Don't retell the author's life story.** No first-person memoir. The mentor voice can say
   "I've seen…" sparingly, but these are teaching lessons, not autobiography.
5. **Age-appropriate.** Teens 11–18. Keep examples relatable to that age (first jobs, phones,
   social media, friends, after-school, college/trades choices).

## Recurring ideas/motifs to weave in naturally (the book's DNA — use the IDEAS, not the story)
- **Future You is a real person** — decisions today are made *for* the adult your kid becomes.
- **Compounding / "stacking"** — small things repeated don't add up, they multiply. Money,
  skills, habits, reputation all compound.
- **The gap** — "money in, minus money out, equals what you keep." The gap is the score, not income.
- **Needs first, then wants** — protect needs before fun; plan fun on purpose.
- **Value-first, not pay-first** — become valuable, and the reward follows (applies to work AND school).
- **Reputation travels** — do what you say you'll do, 100% of the time.
- **Time is the superpower** — starting young beats starting with more money.
- **Want less, win more** — contentment and a wide gap buy freedom; resist lifestyle creep and the
  social-media highlight reel.
- **Borrow other people's hindsight** — ask for advice; learn from people already living it.
- **Celebrate small victories** — count wins, not missed checkpoints.
- **Pay a little now, or a lot later** — the cost of waiting.

## Required structure for EVERY lesson (use this exact skeleton)

```
# Week N — <Lesson Title>

*<One-line italic hook / promise of the week.>*

**This week's big idea:** <1–2 sentences stating the single core takeaway in plain language.>

## <Section heading 1>
<teaching content>

## <Section heading 2>
<teaching content>

## (more sections as needed — aim for 3–5 short sections)

## Try this together
<A concrete, doable activity or conversation the parent can run with their kid THIS week.
Make it specific and low-friction — a script, a 15-minute exercise, a small challenge.>

## Questions to sit with
**For you (the parent):**
- <1–3 reflective questions for the parent themselves>

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- <2–4 questions to ask the teen, or to answer together — tuned to this lesson's content>

---
*Next week: <one-line teaser of the next lesson's title/topic>.*
```

## Length & quality bar
- **Thorough but tight: roughly 900–1,300 words** of actual teaching (not counting questions).
- 3–5 short sections with clear subheads.
- The "Try this together" must be genuinely actionable this week — a script, a worksheet-style
  exercise, a challenge, or a specific conversation. Not vague ("talk about money").
- Questions must be specific to the lesson's content, not generic. Mix parent-reflection and
  kid-facing questions as the topic calls for (some lessons lean more one way — that's fine).
- Markdown only. No tables unless genuinely helpful. Use **bold** for key terms, sparingly.

## Tone examples (match this register)
- GOOD: "Here's the part nobody tells you: your kid won't remember the lecture. They'll remember
  the Saturday you opened a spreadsheet together and it wasn't scary."
- GOOD: "You're not trying to raise a millionaire by Tuesday. You're trying to raise someone who
  knows where their money goes. That's the whole game this week."
- BAD (too preachy): "It is imperative that parents instill fiscal responsibility in their
  progeny from an early age."
- BAD (memoir): "When I was thirty-one and sitting in a jail cell…" ← never do this.
