This week's big idea: Compound interest isn't magic. It's a mechanism. Once your kid understands how it actually works, they'll stop thinking of it as a vague concept and start seeing it as a tool they control. The secret lever isn't the money. It's time, every time.

Money That Earns Money That Earns Money

Let's slow down and make this really plain.

When you put money somewhere it can grow, a savings account, an investment account, it earns a return. Let's say 7% in a year. That's the interest.

Here's the normal part: you put in $100, it earns $7, you now have $107. Makes sense.

Here's the compound part: next year, you're not earning 7% on the original $100. You're earning 7% on $107. So instead of $7, you earn $7.49. That extra 49 cents sounds like nothing. But now your total is $114.49. And next year you'll earn 7% on that. And the year after, on the year after's total.

It's not the money earning money. It's the money earning money earning money earning money. Each year's return becomes part of the base that earns next year's return. The pile grows, the pile grows faster, the pile grows faster still.

In year one, this is boring. In year ten, you notice it. In year thirty, it's the whole story.

(Quick reminder: these examples are illustrative. Actual investment returns vary. They can be lower, higher, or negative in any given period. This is about understanding the concept, not predicting outcomes. Before investing real money, please consult a financial professional.)

The Rule of 72. A Shortcut That Sticks

There's a simple mental trick that makes compounding tangible in about ten seconds, and it's called the Rule of 72.

Here's how it works: divide 72 by your annual rate of return, and you get the approximate number of years it takes for your money to double.

At 6% → 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years to double. At 8% → 72 ÷ 8 = 9 years to double. At 10% → 72 ÷ 10 = 7.2 years to double.

That's it. No calculator needed.

Now think about what doubling means over a long timeline. If your kid puts $1,000 away at 16 and gets an average 8% return, it doubles roughly every 9 years. By 25, it's $2,000. By 34, it's $4,000. By 43, it's $8,000. By 52, it's $16,000. By 60, it's approaching $32,000. From that original $1,000 they put in once.

They didn't touch it. They didn't add a dollar. Time doubled it five times.

Now imagine they add a little every month on top of that. The doublings keep happening on a bigger and bigger base. That's what the calculator showed you last week, but now you can see the why behind the number.

The Three Levers. And Which One Wins Every Time

When you play with a compound interest calculator, you can change three things:

1. How much you put in (the monthly or starting contribution) 2. The rate of return (what growth you assume. This you can't fully control) 3. Time (how many years it grows. This is the one you can control most)

Most people assume the first lever is the most powerful. If I could just put in more money, the result would be so much bigger. And yes, it matters. But it doesn't win.

Time wins. Almost always.

Here's why: a small amount invested for a long time will almost always beat a large amount invested for a short time. That's not opinion. It's arithmetic. Compounding is exponential, which means the later years are when the growth goes vertical. If you cut off the last ten years, you don't lose a little. You lose the part where it goes parabolic.

This is the insight that separates people who build wealth from people who spend their whole lives trying to earn their way into it. The earners think income is the lever. The builders know time is the lever. And your kid is holding time right now, for free, just by being young.

Why People Don't Do This (Hint: It Feels Like Nothing)

You might be wondering: if this is so powerful and so simple, why doesn't everyone do it?

Because doing nothing feels like nothing.

Putting $20 into an account and walking away doesn't feel productive. There's no immediate reward. The account doesn't look exciting. In month one, the return is a few cents. The brain says: "This isn't working. I should do something else."

That "something else" is usually spending the $20 on something visible. Something that gives a hit of satisfaction right now. Which is a completely human impulse. Just an expensive one, compounded over decades.

The people who build wealth early aren't smarter or luckier or more disciplined in some heroic way. They're people who figured out, or were taught, that the boring account growing quietly in the background is actually the most exciting thing happening in their financial life. It just takes patience to see it.

Your kid doesn't have to be a prodigy. They don't have to be a math person. They just have to start, not stop, and not touch it. That's the whole method.

Small + Long Beats Big + Short: Making It a Gut Feeling

Here's the takeaway you want to stick:

Small contributions over a long time will beat large contributions over a short time.

The person who puts in $50 a month from age 20 to age 60 will likely end up with far more than the person who puts in $500 a month from age 40 to age 60. The second person contributed six times more money and ended up behind.

Read that again. Six times more money. Still behind.

That's the gut punch this lesson is designed to deliver. In the most useful possible way. Because if your kid can feel that truth in their body, not just understand it in their head, they'll make different choices. Not out of fear or obligation. Out of genuinely seeing how the game works.

Try this together

Open up the compound interest calculator from last week (or find a new one. Search "compound interest calculator").

This time, rather than running pre-set scenarios, let your kid drive. Give them these specific challenges:

Challenge 1. Find the crossing point. Set two scenarios side by side:

  • Scenario A: $30/month, starting at your kid's current age, for 40 years.
  • Scenario B: $150/month, starting at age 30, for the remaining years until the same end date.

Which one ends higher? (It's usually Scenario A. The early, smaller one.)

Challenge 2. Rule of 72 in action. Start with $1,000 at an 8% rate. Using the Rule of 72, predict when it will double. Then run the calculator to check. (They'll be close. That's the point.)

Challenge 3. The time question. Keep everything the same, but slide the start age earlier by 5 years. By how much does the final number change? Now slide it later by 5 years. Notice what that cost. Let them say the answer out loud: "Starting 5 years earlier was worth ___."

Close the exercise by asking one question together: looking at all of this, what's the single most powerful move your kid could make before they turn 20? Let them answer in their own words. The answer you're hoping to hear: start something, anything, as early as possible and leave it alone.

As always: these exercises are for learning, not financial advice. Returns shown in calculators are illustrative assumptions. Check with a financial professional before making real investment decisions.

Questions to sit with

For yourself:

  • Of the three levers, how much you contribute, the rate, and time, which one have you actually been pulling in your own financial life, and which one have you been ignoring?
  • Run the Rule of 72 on your own savings or investments: how long until your money doubles at the return you're realistically earning. And are you comfortable with that pace?
  • Is there money sitting somewhere "safe" but barely growing that you could move into something that compounds, or a small monthly amount you could start today and not touch for a decade?

For you (the parent):

  • Which lever surprised you most. The amount, the rate, or the time? What does that tell you about your own money decisions?
  • Is there a compounding habit you've already built in your own life, financial or otherwise, that you could point to as proof this works?
  • What do you wish you'd started five or ten years earlier?

For your kid (ask them, or do it together):

  • Using the Rule of 72, how long would it take $1,000 to double at 6%? (See if they can do it in their head.)
  • Which is more powerful: adding $50 more per month, or starting 5 years sooner? What does that tell you?
  • What's one thing in your life, not just money, but skills or habits, that's been compounding quietly while you weren't paying much attention to it?
  • If you had to start one small thing this month that "future you" would thank you for, what would it be?
