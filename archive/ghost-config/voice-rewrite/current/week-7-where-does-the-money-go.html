This week's big idea: "I think I spent about..." is not a money plan. Tracking, even simple, imperfect tracking, gives your kid the one thing that makes every other money skill possible: the truth about where the money actually went.

The Gap Between What We Think and What Actually Happened

Here's a small experiment you can try right now. Before you look at any bank statements, estimate what you spent last week. Not a detailed list. Just a rough total. Food, gas or transportation, small purchases, a coffee or snack. Hold that number in your head.

Now look at the actual transactions.

For most people, there's a gap. Not because they're irresponsible. Because spending is often invisible while it's happening. A small purchase here, a tap-to-pay there, a subscription charge that comes through automatically. None of it feels like much in the moment. The number at the end of the month tells a different story.

Your teen has the same experience on a smaller scale. Ask them where their money went last week and they'll give you a reasonable-sounding answer. Pull up the actual transactions, or just ask them to account for it dollar by dollar, and the picture often shifts.

That shift isn't a moral failing. It's just the difference between guessing and knowing. And knowing is where every real financial decision starts.

Why Tracking Works When Budgeting Alone Doesn't

A budget is a plan. Tracking is the check on whether the plan held.

You can build the best budget in the world and still be surprised at the end of the month. Because plans are made when you're calm and thinking clearly, and spending happens in moments. At checkout, when you're hungry, when a friend wants to go somewhere, when an app sends a notification at exactly the right moment.

Tracking closes that loop. When your teen knows they're going to write down every purchase, even informally, even in a notes app, something shifts. Not because they become more disciplined by magic, but because awareness changes behavior. The $4 impulse buy looks different when you know you'll write it down in five minutes.

This isn't surveillance. It's clarity. And the earlier a kid develops the reflex to track, even loosely, the less likely they are to spend years wondering where their money went.

The Small Leaks Are Usually the Story

When adults track their spending seriously for the first time, there's almost always one discovery that surprises them. Rarely is it the big planned expenses. Rent, car payment, groceries. Those are visible. The surprise is almost always the small, steady stuff.

Streaming subscriptions that haven't been used in months. Coffee or snacks that add up to $80 or $100 by the end of the month. App purchases that felt like nothing. Online impulse buys under $15 that happened four times in a week.

For teens, the pattern is similar. The $3 purchase at the school snack bar, every day. The in-app upgrades. The "I'll just get this one thing" on the way somewhere. None of it feels like a decision. All of it shows up in the total.

This is where the value of tracking really lives. Not in tracking the big stuff you already knew about, but in surfacing the small, invisible stuff that has quietly been running in the background.

When your teen sees their first week of tracked spending, the small leaks will probably be in there. That's the lesson. Not "don't buy snacks". But "here's where the money actually went, so now you can choose."

Simple Tracking Methods That Actually Work

The enemy of tracking is friction. If the system is annoying, it stops. So the goal is to find a method that's simple enough to maintain.

A few options that genuinely work:

The notes app. Every time money leaves, cash, card, app, they type it in. Just the amount and what it was. Nothing fancy. Review it together at the end of the week.

A small notebook. Old-fashioned, but it works. Some teens actually prefer writing it down. The physical act helps it register.

A shared spreadsheet. If your teen is inclined this way, a simple Google Sheet with two columns, date and amount, is enough. You can even share it with them and track your own spending in the same document, which is one of the most effective things you can do together this week.

Screenshot or bank app review. For teens with a debit card, simply pulling up the transaction list and going through it together at the end of the week works too. It's slightly less real-time than writing it down, but it still reveals the full picture.

Pick the one with the least friction. A simple system they'll actually use beats a detailed one they'll abandon.

You Do It Too. This Week

Here's the part that matters most: do it alongside them.

Seven days. Every dollar, for both of you. Then compare notes at the end.

When a parent tracks their spending and shows their kid the results, openly, without spin, it does something no lecture can do. It says: this is a real skill that real adults use. It's not a punishment. It's just how people who manage money well actually manage money.

The conversation at the end of the week doesn't need to be long. "Here's what I found" from you, and "here's what I found" from them, and then a few minutes looking at it together. What surprised you? What makes sense? What might you do differently?

That's the whole exercise. But the week you actually track, both of you, builds more awareness than months of talking about it.

Try this together

This week's challenge: a 7-day spending log. Both of you. Every dollar.

How to set it up: 1. Each of you picks a tracking method. Notes app, notebook, bank app screenshots, or a shared spreadsheet. Pick the same one if that's easier. 2. Starting today, log every single purchase. Small ones count. Cash counts. App purchases count. Even splitting a meal with someone counts. 3. At the end of the 7 days, sit down together with your logs.

What to do with what you find:

  • Add up the total spent for the week.
  • Sort purchases roughly into categories. Food, entertainment, transportation, random stuff.
  • Name the one item that surprised you most. The leak you didn't see coming.
  • Notice: did the act of logging change anything about how you spent during the week?

No grading. No judgment. Just information. The goal this week is awareness, not perfection.

Questions to sit with

For yourself:

  • If you guessed what you spent last week and then pulled up the actual transactions, how big do you think the gap between the two would be?
  • Which tracking method has the least friction for you, notes app, notebook, or just a weekly bank review, and would you actually keep it up for seven days?
  • What's the small, steady leak you suspect is quietly running in the background of your spending, a subscription, a daily coffee, the under-$15 impulse buys, that you've never actually added up?

For you (the parent):

  • What would your spending log reveal that you didn't expect. And what would you want to do about it?
  • Are there subscriptions or small recurring charges in your spending that have crept in unnoticed?

For your kid (ask them, or do it together):

  • When you look at the week's log, where did your money actually go. And does that match where you thought it went?
  • What was the small leak that surprised you most?
  • Did writing things down change how you thought about any purchase before you made it?
  • If you had to cut one thing from this list to widen your gap, what would it be?
