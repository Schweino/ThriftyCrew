This week's big idea: There's no single "right" system for money at home, but every system sends a message. The goal isn't to pick the perfect model. It's to choose your model on purpose and be consistent about it.

Every Family Already Has a Money System

Whether you've thought about it or not, your household already teaches your teen something about how money works. If you hand them a set amount every week regardless of what they did, that's a lesson. If they only get money when they ask for it, that's a lesson. If money is connected to chores, or grades, or effort. That's a lesson too.

None of these are automatically wrong. But all of them are working on your kid all the time, teaching them how money flows in relation to behavior, effort, and expectations. The parents who get the most out of this stage are the ones who make that system deliberate. Who decide what lesson they're trying to teach and then build a model around it.

This week isn't about finding the one right answer. It's about looking clearly at what your current setup actually teaches, and adjusting where it's off.

The Three Basic Models

Most families end up somewhere in one of these three camps, or blending them:

Pure allowance. A fixed amount, given regularly, tied to nothing in particular. Your teen gets $20 a week because they're part of the family and you want them to have money to practice with. The lesson here: money is something people give you. Membership has its perks. The risk: no connection between money and effort. The upside: simplicity, and your kid can focus on learning to manage the money rather than earning it.

Commission-based (or "earned") model. Money is only available in exchange for specific tasks. Complete the list, get paid; skip the tasks, nothing comes in. The lesson here: work and money are directly linked. The risk: some families find this creates a transactional kid who won't do anything without getting paid for it. Including things that should just be part of living together. The upside: it mirrors how the adult world actually works.

Hybrid. Some money is given as a baseline (because they're part of the family), and some is available to earn on top through optional or extra tasks. The lesson here: some things are expected just because we're a family. And there are also opportunities to earn more through effort. This tends to be the most flexible and the most forgiving of inconsistency, which is why a lot of families default to something like it.

None of these descriptions is meant to sell you on a model. They're meant to help you see what's already happening and whether it lines up with what you're trying to teach.

The Chore Problem

Here's where a lot of families get tangled: tying every household chore to a specific payment amount.

The concern is this. When every action has a dollar sign attached, you can accidentally teach your teen that their contribution to the household is optional, as long as they're willing to forgo the cash. "I don't need the money this week, so I'm not washing the dishes." That's probably not the value you were going for.

Most family therapists and parenting educators who have wrestled with this suggest distinguishing between two categories:

Baseline responsibilities. Things every family member does because they live here and we all contribute. Keeping their room livable, clearing their dishes, taking out the trash when it's their turn. These don't get paid. They get expected.

Earning opportunities. Tasks above the baseline that are genuinely optional and have a real dollar value attached. Mowing the yard, washing the car, organizing the garage, babysitting a sibling for an evening. These are fair to compensate.

The line between the two is your call. Every family draws it differently. But drawing it clearly, and saying it out loud to your teen, removes a lot of the confusion and resentment on both sides.

Consistency Is the Whole Game

Whatever model you land on, the thing that actually makes it work is doing it the same way every time.

Teens aren't great at unpredictability when it comes to money. If the rules shift based on your mood, their attitude, how the week went, or whether you remembered. The lesson they absorb is that money is arbitrary and shows up or doesn't based on forces they can't control. That's not a great foundation for building saving habits, spending awareness, or any of the other things we're trying to teach.

This doesn't mean the system can't ever change. It just means that changes should be announced, explained, and consistent going forward. Not improvised week to week.

It also means that if the current system isn't working, it's worth a real conversation with your teen to redesign it together. Teens are much more likely to buy into a system they helped shape than one that was handed to them. Which brings us to the activity.

What to Do If the Current System Isn't Working

You might read this and realize that the current money setup at home is... kind of a mess. Inconsistent payments, unclear expectations, tension around chores, no real savings behavior being developed. That's fine. That's most families. You don't have to fix it all at once.

Start with one change. The best first change is usually clarity: write down what the expectations are, what gets paid, what doesn't, and how much. Bring your teen into the room and have the conversation. It doesn't have to be a formal meeting. It can happen at dinner or in the car. The goal is just that everyone knows what the system is.

Compounding applies here too. A simple, consistent, clearly understood money system repeated over months and years does more for your teen than an elaborate perfect system that nobody follows.

Try this together

This week, design (or revise) your family's money model. On one page, in writing.

Sit down with your teen and map out three things:

1. What's given: Is there a baseline amount that comes regularly, regardless of tasks? How much, and when? 2. What's earned: What optional tasks are available to earn extra? What does each one pay? 3. What's expected for free: What are the household responsibilities every family member does just because we're a family. No money attached?

Write these down. A notes app works, a piece of paper on the fridge works, whatever you'll actually look at. The format doesn't matter. The clarity does.

If you already have a system that's working, use this time to put it in writing anyway. Saying it out loud and seeing it written makes it real in a different way. For both of you.

Ask your teen if they think it's fair. You don't have to agree with their answer, but hearing it tells you something.

Questions to sit with

For yourself:

  • In your own life, what counts as a baseline you handle no matter what, and what's genuinely optional or extra? Have you ever drawn that line on paper, or does it shift with your mood?
  • Where is your relationship with your own money inconsistent. Saving hard one month, letting it all slide the next? What single rule, applied the same way every time, would steady it?
  • Do you ever treat your effort as transactional, holding back unless there's an obvious payoff? Where might showing up consistently, paid or not, actually be the thing that compounds?

For you (the parent):

  • What is your current system actually teaching your teen. About the relationship between effort and money, about expectations, about what they're owed? Is that what you meant to teach?
  • How consistent have you actually been? And what one change would make the system easier to stick with?

For your kid (ask them, or do it together):

  • Looking at the system we just wrote down. What feels fair? What doesn't?
  • Is there a task you'd be willing to do regularly to earn more, if it paid the right amount? What would that look like?
  • What do you think you should do around the house just because we're a family. Without getting paid for it?
