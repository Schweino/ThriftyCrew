This week's big idea: Student loans are not automatic doom. But they are a serious commitment that deserves serious thought before signing. The families who come out ahead are the ones who look at the real numbers early, borrow only what's truly needed, and don't wait until graduation to start chipping away.

The Number Nobody Wants to Say Out Loud

Let's start by naming what we're actually talking about. The average student borrower in the United States graduates with somewhere north of $30,000 in debt. That's not pocket change. For some fields and some schools, it's two or three times that.

And here's the part that catches folks off guard. The interest doesn't wait politely until you graduate to start running. On most unsubsidized loans, interest piles up the whole time your kid is in school. They graduate, and the balance is already bigger than the original loan. That's not a punishment. It's just math. The kind of math that rewards people who understand it and punishes people who don't look at it until it's too late.

That's why this conversation happens now, before any loan paperwork gets signed. Not because loans are shameful. Not because college isn't worth it. But because informed families make smarter decisions.

College Is Not the Only Path. And That's Worth Saying Clearly

Before we get into how loans work, let's spend one honest minute here. College is one excellent road. It is not the only road.

Skilled trades, electricians, plumbers, HVAC technicians, welders, dental hygienists, are desperately needed, often pay extremely well, and frequently take two years or less of training at a fraction of the cost of a four-year degree. If your kid is drawn to hands-on work, running their own shop, or a field that doesn't require a specific credential, it's worth genuinely exploring that path rather than defaulting to the idea that four years of college is non-negotiable.

This isn't about talking a kid out of ambition. It's about matching the investment to the return, and to the kid in front of you. A student who heads to college with a clear goal and a plan to keep debt low is in a very different spot than one who's there because it seemed like the next step.

How Student Loans Actually Work

Here's the simple version.

Federal student loans come from the government. They usually carry lower interest rates and more repayment flexibility than private loans. They come in two main types. Subsidized, where the government covers the interest while you're in school if you qualify based on financial need, and unsubsidized, where interest starts building right away. Private loans come from banks and other lenders. They can have higher rates and fewer protections.

The FAFSA, Free Application for Federal Student Aid, is the form that decides what federal aid your kid qualifies for. Fill it out early, every year they're in school. It unlocks grants, which are free money with no repayment, plus work-study opportunities and subsidized loans. This is not optional paperwork. It's the front door to a real amount of help.

When loan offers show up, read them. The interest rate, the loan type, and the total borrowed amount are the numbers that matter. Accepting the maximum offered loan "just in case" is how families end up with debt they didn't need.

The Rule That Changes Everything: Don't Wait Until Graduation

Here's the single most actionable thing in this entire lesson.

If your kid works at all during school, a part-time job, a summer gig, anything, and they put even a modest amount toward their loan interest while they're still in school, they graduate in a fundamentally different position than their peers.

Not making full payments. Not heroic sacrifice. Just paying the interest as it builds, or more when they can, so the balance doesn't quietly balloon. Even $50 or $100 a month during the school years keeps the original amount from swelling. It's the compounding principle in reverse. Stop the interest from stacking, and the payoff timeline shrinks dramatically.

This is the kind of move Future You will thank you for. The twenty-two-year-old who graduates with roughly what they borrowed, instead of $5,000–$10,000 more, has a head start that matters.

The Return-on-Investment Conversation

Here's a useful frame for any specific school and major your kid is weighing. What's the realistic starting salary in that field, and how does it compare to the total cost of attending that school for four years?

This is not a cold, purely financial calculation. There are real reasons to attend a school that costs more, or to chase a field that doesn't pay the most. But those decisions should be made with eyes open, not by default because a school looked great on a campus visit.

A rough rule of thumb many financial educators suggest: total student loan debt at graduation should ideally stay below the first year's expected salary in that field. So if a field typically pays $40,000 to start, borrowing $80,000 for school is a very different conversation than borrowing $35,000. That's not a hard line. It's a thinking tool. Use it.

Scholarships, grants, community college for the first two years, in-state tuition, and living at home can all dramatically cut the total borrowed. These options aren't consolation prizes. They're smart moves that Future You will appreciate enormously.

Try this together

If college is anywhere on your kid's radar, even a few years out, do this exercise together this week. Pick one school your kid has mentioned or is curious about. Look up the total annual cost, tuition, fees, room, board, the real number. Then look up the median starting salary for the field your kid is considering. You can find both with a basic search.

Write down two numbers side by side. Total cost for four years versus likely first-year salary. Now ask: if we borrowed everything, how long would it take to pay off? And what would we need to do, scholarships, working during school, starting at a community college, to close that gap?

You don't need answers tonight. The goal is to make the conversation real and number-based, not abstract. "College is expensive" is something a teen can shrug off. A specific dollar figure sitting next to a specific salary is something they can actually think about.

Questions to sit with

For yourself:

  • If you're carrying student loans now, do you actually know the rate, the type, and the total balance on each one? Or have you been making payments without ever looking at the real numbers?
  • Whether you're eyeing more schooling, a certificate, or a career pivot, does the cost line up with the realistic income it leads to? Or are you about to borrow more than that first year would pay?
  • Is there a "chip away now instead of waiting" move available to you, even $50 or $100 a month toward interest or principal, that would shrink your payoff timeline more than you'd expect?

For you (the parent):

  • What do you wish you had known about student loans before you (or your kid) started making decisions about school?
  • Have you had a concrete conversation with your kid about the actual cost of the schools they're interested in? Or has it mostly been hopes and rankings?
  • Are there options, trades, community college, work-study, scholarships, you haven't fully explored together yet?

For your kid (ask them, or do it together):

  • If you had to describe in one sentence what you want your life to look like at 25, what would you say? What path gets you there?
  • What's one thing you could do during the school years to make sure you graduate with as little debt as possible?
  • By the real definition of "affordable," can you afford the school you're most excited about right now? And if not, what's the plan to make it work?
