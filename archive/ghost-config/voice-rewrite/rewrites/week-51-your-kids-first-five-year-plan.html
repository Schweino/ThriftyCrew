This week's big idea: Big dreams are great. Your kid needs them. But the work always lives in the small daily steps. A one-page five-year plan ties the dream at the top to the tiny first move at the bottom, and that connection is what makes real progress possible.

Why Plans Feel Intimidating (and Why They Shouldn't)

When most folks hear "five-year plan," they picture a formal document. Polished, detailed, and somehow already wrong by next Thursday. That version is intimidating, and it should be, because it's not what we're building.

What we're building is a lot simpler. It's a one-page document with a big dream at the top and a small first step at the bottom. It doesn't have to be perfect. It doesn't have to be final. It just has to be specific enough to point in a direction. And honest enough to name what the next tiny move looks like.

For a teenager, this is genuinely powerful. Most teens run on a pretty short time horizon by default. The future is abstract. "Future You" (remember Week 1?) can feel like a cartoon character with no real connection to the kid standing in front of you. A written plan makes that connection visible. It turns a vague wish into an actual intention. And intentions are a whole lot easier to act on.

You're not locking in a career, committing to a university, or promising a salary. You're just answering, roughly, what direction? And what's one thing I can do about it this week?

Dream Big at the Top. Then Work Down

Here's the structure that works. It has six layers, and you move from the big to the small.

1. The Big Dream This one's allowed to be ambitious. If your kid could fast-forward five years and peek at the life they'd love to be living, what do they see? Where are they? What are they doing? What does their financial situation look like, roughly? Don't trim this down to what seems "realistic." Let it be real first.

2. The Direction This is the general path. Not a specific job title, but a direction: building something, working with people, working with their hands, creating, solving problems, going to college, learning a trade, starting a side business. The direction doesn't have to be crystal clear. Fuzzy is fine. The question is just, which general way am I facing?

3. This Year's Goals Three or four specific things for the next 12 months. Not dreams. Goals. Things you can check off. "Get a part-time job." "Save $X." "Research two schools/programs I'm interested in." "Learn one real skill." These should feel doable but ask for real effort.

4. Tiny First Steps This is the most important layer. For each of this year's goals, what's the smallest possible action that moves toward it? The tiniest first step. Not "figure out my whole career." "look up two programs online for 20 minutes on Sunday." Not "save $1,000." "open the savings account and move $20 this week." Tiny. Doable. Right now.

5. Who to Ask for Help A plan built alone is a weaker plan. Who does your kid know, or who could they meet, that's already living something close to this direction? One or two names. What's one question they could ask? This is borrowed hindsight in action. You skip years of trial and error by talking to somebody who's already done it.

6. How You'll Celebrate Wins Because this whole year has been about counting victories, not missing checkpoints. Pick two or three milestones in the year ahead and name how you'll celebrate when you hit them. Small wins deserve acknowledgment. That acknowledgment is the fuel.

The Plan Isn't the Point. The Habit Is

Here's something worth telling your teen: the act of making a plan matters more than having the perfect plan.

Plans change. Directions shift. The thing your kid is fired up about today might not be what grabs them in three years, and that's completely normal. This is not a binding contract with the future. It's a current-best-answer, made with the information you've got right now.

What the plan does is give you a reference point. When choices come up, which class to take, whether to apply for a job, how to spend the summer, there's something to check them against. "Does this move me toward my direction, or away from it?" That one question, asked on a regular basis, cuts through a lot of noise.

And revisiting the plan, even once a year, is a valuable habit all by itself. People who write down goals and check them now and then don't hit everything they wrote. But they hit far more than folks who never wrote anything down at all.

Don't Plan Alone

If there's one thing to repeat from the last several weeks, it's this: your kid doesn't have to figure this out from scratch.

Somebody is already living something close to what they're aiming for. Somebody has already made the mistakes your kid is about to make, already picked up the skills they'll need, already learned which paths are dead ends. Asking that person, one hour over coffee or a message online, is worth months of solo trial and error.

Help your kid put one real name on the plan. Not a celebrity or a vague idea, but an actual person: a relative, a friend's parent, someone from a mentor program, a former coach, a teacher. One person whose life or career even roughly points the same way. And help them draft one honest question to ask.

That conversation might not happen right away. But putting the name on the plan turns it into a real intention instead of a "someday I should probably talk to somebody" that never happens.

A Note for You, the Parent

Worth saying plainly: your kid watching you fill out this plan right alongside them beats any lecture about goal-setting you could ever give. If you have a five-year plan, even a rough one, even an uncertain one, share it. If you don't, this is a good week to sketch one out.

You don't need to fake a certainty you don't have. "I'm not totally sure, but the direction I'm heading is..." is a completely honest and useful thing to say. Showing that plans can be imperfect and still useful is one of the most helpful things you can model. Been there myself, more than once.

Try this together

Sit down together this week and fill out the template above. One for your kid, and ideally one for yourself too. This doesn't need to be a long session. An hour is plenty. You're not after a polished document. You're after a real conversation that ends with something written down.

A few tips for the session. Start with the Big Dream before you touch anything else. Let it be big. Fight the urge to immediately problem-solve or reality-check it. The editing comes later. The dreaming comes first. Then work your way down to the tiny first steps, which is where most of the session's energy should land. Because those are the ones that actually happen.

When you're done, put the plan somewhere visible. Fridge, bedroom door, a folder they'll actually open. Not buried in a notebook. And set a specific date to check in on it. Six months from now is a good target.

Questions to sit with

For yourself:

  • If you fast-forward five years to the life you'd love to be living, what does it actually look like. And have you ever let yourself name the big dream without editing it down to "realistic"?
  • For the direction you're heading, what's one tiny first step, something you could do in the next 20 minutes this week, that you keep putting off?
  • Who do you know who's already living something close to where you want to go, and what's one honest question you could ask them?

For you (the parent):

  • What is your own five-year direction, roughly? Have you written it down? If not, this is the week.
  • What would you have done differently at your kid's age if someone had sat down and helped you make a plan like this?

For your kid (ask them, or do it together):

  • What's the big dream. Not the "realistic" version, the real one?
  • If you had to name the very first tiny step you could take this week toward one of your goals, what would it be?
  • Who's one person living something close to what you want? What would you ask them?
  • How will you know this year has been a good one? What would make you proud to look back on?
