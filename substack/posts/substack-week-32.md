**This week's big idea:** Debt doesn't usually arrive looking like a threat — it arrives looking like a favor, a convenience, or a reward. Teaching your teen to see the costume before they open the door is one of the most practical money lessons you can give them.

## The Kindest-Looking Danger

Think about the last time someone offered you something that felt like genuine help. A store employee who noticed you loved a jacket and mentioned there's an easy way to take it home today — just sign up for the card, and you get 10% off right now. An app that offered to split a purchase into four easy payments over six weeks, so it didn't hit all at once. A credit card with rewards points, no annual fee, and a zero-interest introductory period that lasts 12 months.

None of those felt like warnings. They felt like good deals. They felt like whoever was offering them was on your side.

That is, without question, the most important thing to understand about debt traps: they are engineered to feel like favors. The language is helpful. The timing is perfect. The friction is almost nonexistent. Everything is designed to make "yes" feel like the obvious, comfortable, grateful thing to do.

Your kid is going to encounter these offers constantly. In stores, online, in apps, in their college mailbox, at their first job. And the people delivering them are very, very good at their jobs.

You're not trying to make your teen paranoid. You're trying to give them the lens to see the costume — so when the offer arrives, they can pause for a second and ask: *wait, who's this actually good for?*

## How the Costume Works

The mechanics are worth understanding, because once your teen sees how the trick is done, it's harder to fall for it.

The "favor" version of a financial trap usually works on one or more of the following:

**It makes something unaffordable feel affordable.** "Only $29 a month" sounds like nothing. But $29 a month for 24 months is $696 — and that's before interest. The monthly framing shrinks the real number to the point where the brain stops doing math. This is intentional.

**It delays the pain.** Buy now, pay later. Zero interest for 12 months. No payments until spring. The gap between the pleasure of getting the thing and the reality of paying for it is stretched as far as possible. By the time the bill arrives, the excitement is long gone — but the balance isn't.

**It comes with a reward.** Points, cash back, a signup discount, a free gift. These feel like the company is giving something away. They're not. The rewards are built into the interest rates and fees that most cardholders will pay. The people who pay interest every month are subsidizing the perks that get marketed to everyone.

**It makes saying yes easy and saying no awkward.** You're at the register. There's a line behind you. The cashier is friendly. "Do you want to save 20% today by opening our card?" Saying no feels rude. The offer is right there. You're already getting the thing. Of course you say yes.

None of this is accidental. It's a finely tuned system that has been refined over decades of psychology and data. Your teen isn't fighting a bad offer — they're fighting an industry that knows more about human behavior than most people realize.

## The Question That Cuts Through It

Here's a simple filter your kid can use for the rest of their life.

When an offer sounds like a favor, ask: *if I couldn't use credit — if I had to pay for this in full, right now, with money I already have — would I still do it?*

If the answer is no, then it's not something they can afford. The payment plan didn't make it affordable. It made an unaffordable thing feel like it fit.

This question applies to the store credit card, the buy-now-pay-later split, the "0% financing for 18 months" on a new laptop. If the only way the math works is because payments are spread out — and paying in full would mean not getting it — then the trap is already open.

The answer isn't always no. Sometimes credit used wisely makes sense. But that conversation starts with honesty about the full price, not comfort with the monthly fragment.

## It's Not About Willpower — It's About Seeing It Coming

One thing that doesn't help: telling a teen that they just need to "be stronger" or "have more discipline." This framing puts all the weight on the individual and ignores that these systems are purpose-built to be hard to resist.

A smarter frame is awareness. People who avoid debt traps aren't necessarily tougher — they're just earlier to recognize the pattern. They've seen the costume before. When they hear "easy payments" or "no interest if paid in full," they don't feel warmth — they feel the pull of a well-designed system, and they choose deliberately instead of automatically.

That's the gift you're giving your kid this week. Not willpower training. Pattern recognition.

Once they see the structure — once they know that every "favor" offer has a business model behind it, and that business model depends on them paying more than the price tag — they can engage with the offer on their own terms instead of the offer's terms.

## Small Charges, Long Chain

One more thing worth naming: it's usually not one big trap. It's a slow accumulation of small ones that each felt reasonable at the time.

A streaming subscription. A credit card opened for the one-time discount. A buy-now-pay-later split on a pair of shoes. A gym membership auto-renewed. None of these, alone, look like a problem. Together, they're $200 a month going out the door in small, mostly invisible chunks — before rent, before groceries, before any of the things that actually matter.

The gap narrows a little here, a little there, until one day the paycheck runs out before the month does, and nobody can explain exactly why.

Teaching your teen to spot the individual trap is good. Teaching them to also audit the collection — all the small commitments at once — is even better.

## Try this together

This week, go trap-spotting together. Aim for at least three examples.

Look at: store checkout lines (physical or online), apps on your teen's phone (especially anything with a subscription or "upgrade" prompt), ads in their social media feed, emails in your inbox, or the receipt from a recent purchase.

For each one you find, ask: What's the costume? What does it look and sound like it's offering? What does it actually cost over time? Who benefits more — the buyer or the seller?

Keep it light and a little fun. You're not trying to make your teen afraid of every financial offer. You're making the costume visible. Once they've decoded a few, they'll start seeing the pattern on their own.

If you find a particularly good example — a "buy now, pay later" option at checkout, a store card signup offer — take a screenshot and look up the actual interest rate together.

## Questions to sit with

**For yourself:**
- What "favor" — a store card, a buy-now-pay-later split, a 0% financing offer — have you said yes to that turned out to cost more than the thing was worth?
- Run the honest test on a recent purchase: if you'd had to pay the full price in cash, right now, with money you already had, would you still have bought it?
- When's the last time you audited all your small recurring charges at once — the subscriptions, memberships, and auto-renewals — and how much do you think is quietly leaving your account every month?

**For you (the parent):**
- What "favor" has cost you the most over your lifetime — looking back, what felt like help that wasn't?
- Do you have any small recurring charges right now that you'd forgotten about until this lesson made you think of them?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- What's an offer you've seen recently that felt like a good deal — and how does it hold up when you ask who it's really good for?
- What's the difference between something being convenient and something being helpful?
- If you couldn't use credit or split payments, what would you have bought differently in the last year?
