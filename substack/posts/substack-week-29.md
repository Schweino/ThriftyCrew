## Why This Conversation Is Yours to Start

Here's something the personal finance industry doesn't say loudly enough: most teens who understand compounding still don't invest a dollar until they're in their mid-twenties. Not because they're lazy. Because no one ever sat down with them and actually opened the account.

The information barrier is low. The action barrier is higher than it looks — and it's almost always because the parent was waiting to feel "ready" or "qualified," and the teen couldn't do it alone anyway.

Your kid genuinely cannot open a standard brokerage account by themselves. They're a minor. They need an adult on the account. That means this isn't optional for you to delegate. If investing is going to happen for your teen, it starts with you.

The good news: you don't need to know everything. You don't need to have it all figured out. You just need to take the first step together.

## What a Custodial Account Actually Is

A custodial account (also called a UGMA or UTMA account, depending on the account type) is a brokerage account that a parent or guardian opens on behalf of a minor. You're the custodian — meaning you manage it until your child reaches the age of majority (usually 18 or 21, depending on your state). At that point, the account legally becomes theirs.

Think of it as a starter investing account with your name on the door until they're old enough to hold the key themselves.

Several major brokerages offer custodial accounts — Fidelity, Charles Schwab, Vanguard, and others have options specifically designed for families. Many of them have no minimum to get started, which matters when the whole point is to begin small and let time do the work.

Some banks and apps have also started offering teen-focused investing accounts (Fidelity Youth Account is one example). These can be a good on-ramp because they're designed to be less intimidating and sometimes include educational content.

You don't need to pick the "perfect" platform. You need to pick one and start.

## The Power of Starting Tiny

This is where a lot of parents get stuck. They think the account isn't worth opening unless there's real money to put in. That thinking is backwards.

The whole point of the last two weeks — the compounding math, the time-is-the-superpower lesson — is that the amount matters far less than the start date. Twenty dollars invested at 16 is worth more to Future Your Kid than $200 invested at 25, given enough time.

The first $20 in that account isn't really about $20. It's about making the concept real. It's about your teen watching a number on a screen tick up and down and learning, in their gut, what it feels like to own a piece of something. It's about building the identity of someone who invests — before the habit is crowded out by rent, car payments, and the ten other things that consume a first paycheck.

That identity is the real investment. The money is almost secondary.

So if you're waiting until you can put in a "meaningful amount," stop waiting. Twenty dollars is meaningful.

## The Conversation to Have (and How to Frame It)

A lot of parents stumble here because they feel like they should have all the answers before they bring it up. You don't. In fact, going into this together — as something you're figuring out alongside your kid — is actually better. It teaches them how to research, compare, and make decisions about money in real time.

Here's a simple way to start the conversation:

*"Hey, I've been thinking about the stuff we've been learning about investing and compounding. You can't open an account on your own yet, but I can open one with you. Want to look at our options together this week?"*

That's it. No big speech needed. You're not a financial advisor. You're a parent who's willing to walk through a door with your kid.

From there, look together. Search "custodial brokerage account" and compare two or three options. Read the basics. Ask: Is there a minimum to start? Is it easy to add money over time? Can your teen see and follow the account? Is there an educational component?

Let your teen be part of the research. When they've helped choose the account, they're more invested (literally and figuratively) in what happens next.

## What Happens After You Open It

Opening the account is the milestone this week. What goes into it — and when — is a conversation for Week 30, when we'll talk about what to actually put the money into. For now, the goal is simply to have the account exist.

Once it's open, set a simple rule together: a percentage of any money your kid earns or receives goes into this account. Ten percent is a great starting point. If they earn $50 mowing a lawn, five dollars goes in. If they get a $50 birthday check, five dollars goes in. Small, automatic, consistent — this is how the habit forms.

You might also consider a match. Not because you have to, but because matching is a powerful teaching tool. If you put in $1 for every $1 they put in, two things happen: the account grows faster, and your teen learns one of the most important financial lessons they'll ever encounter in adult life — the employer match. We'll talk about that in Week 31.

For now, the goal is simple: get the account open.

## Try this together

This week, spend 20–30 minutes researching custodial or teen investing account options together. You can search "best custodial brokerage accounts" or "teen investing accounts" and compare two or three options side by side.

Look at: minimum to open, ease of use, whether your teen can have their own login or view, and any fees. Fidelity Youth Account, Charles Schwab's custodial option, and Vanguard's custodial account are reasonable starting points to compare.

If you feel ready after your research — open the account together. Make it an event, not a chore. If you want a week to think it through, that's fine too. But set a specific "we'll open it by" date before you walk away from the conversation.

If an account already exists, spend the 20–30 minutes reviewing it together, explaining what's in it, and setting the "percentage in" rule.

## Questions to sit with

**For yourself:**
- Do you actually have a brokerage or investing account of your own — and if not, is the barrier practical, or is it the same "I don't feel qualified yet" feeling that keeps so many people on the sidelines?
- If you opened or funded an account this week with even $20, what identity would you be stepping into — and what's really stopping you from claiming "I'm someone who invests"?
- What's one money question you've been guessing about instead of just looking up — and could you sit down and research it this week the way you'd want a kid to?

**For you (the parent):**
- What has been the real barrier to opening an account for your teen — practical, or mostly emotional (not feeling qualified, not knowing where to start)?
- If you don't currently have a brokerage account of your own, would you be willing to open one alongside theirs — and let the parallel track be part of the lesson?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- If I matched every dollar you put in, what amount could you commit to putting in each month — even if it's small?
- What feels scary or confusing about investing right now? Let's look it up together instead of guessing.
- Why do you think most adults wait until their 30s to start investing — and what does that mean for you starting now?
