**This week's big idea:** Some debt, entered carefully and with a clear exit, can be a reasonable tool for building a life. But the thing that turns debt from a tool into a trap isn't the amount — it's the absence of a plan to get out. You can plan into debt in five minutes. Spend at least as much time planning your way back out.

## First, a Word About Shame

Let's get this out of the way before anything else.

If you have debt right now — a car loan, a credit card balance, a student loan, medical bills, whatever it is — this lesson is not a verdict on you. Debt is not a character flaw. It's a financial situation. And financial situations can be understood, managed, and changed.

The same goes for the message you're passing to your teen. If they've picked up the idea that debt means someone did something wrong, that's worth gently correcting. Plenty of thoughtful, hardworking people carry debt. What separates the ones who manage it well from the ones who get buried by it isn't their virtue — it's usually whether they had a plan and understood what they were getting into.

That's what this lesson is about: helping your kid build the habit of thinking clearly about debt before they're in it, so they always have the map and the exit.

## Not All Debt Is the Same

Here's the honest truth: some debt, under the right conditions, makes sense.

A car to get to work. If your teen is 19 and takes a job 25 miles away and needs transportation and has no other way to get there, a modest car loan can be a reasonable investment in their ability to earn. The car is a tool that generates income. As long as the payment fits the income and there's a clear payoff plan, this can work.

A home. Most people will never be able to pay cash for a house. A mortgage — a long-term loan to own a place to live — is a standard part of adult financial life for much of the population. Done carefully (within budget, with enough saved for a down payment), it can be a reasonable long-term decision. It's not free money; interest adds up over decades. But it's a different animal than consumer debt.

Sometimes school. Education can — sometimes — be a reasonable debt if the cost is proportional to what the training leads to. A $20,000 loan to enter a field that starts at $60,000 a year is very different from $120,000 in loans for a degree with unclear income prospects. The math matters, and we'll dig into that more next week on student loans specifically.

The through-line in all of these? Each one has a practical purpose. Each one connects to something that generates income or provides something essential. And each one can, in theory, be paid off on a realistic timeline.

## What Makes Debt a Trap

Debt becomes a trap under a few specific conditions. Help your teen recognize these early:

**No exit plan.** You borrow money without any clear idea of when or how you'll pay it back. You just know the payment fits for now. This is how debt that was "temporary" stays for years.

**No connection to value.** The debt is for something that doesn't produce income, isn't essential, and doesn't hold value over time. Consumer goods, fashion, restaurants, entertainment — financing these things means you're paying interest on something that's already been consumed, and you're paying for it long after you've forgotten how much you enjoyed it.

**The payment trap.** You borrowed based on what the payment felt like, not what the total cost was. (Sound familiar? This is the same trick from Week 33 and Week 35.) A three-year car loan at a high interest rate might have a manageable payment and a painful total cost. The exit plan isn't just "I'll make the payments." It's "I know exactly when this is paid off and what the total I'm paying is."

**Stacking debt on debt.** One manageable payment can become two, then three. Each one feels small. Together, they absorb all the breathing room in a paycheck. Debt stacks; so does the feeling of being trapped by it.

## The Exit Plan Question

Here's the single most useful habit you can help your teen build around debt: before they take on any, ask this one question:

**"What is my plan to get out of this, and by when?"**

Not "can I make the payment?" Not "does it feel okay right now?" The exit plan. Specifically.

If the answer is vague — "I'll pay it off eventually" or "I'll figure it out" — that's a sign the decision needs more thought. A real exit plan has numbers and a timeline. "I'm borrowing $3,000. My payment is $X a month. At that rate, it's paid off in Y months. I know the total I'm paying including interest is $Z."

When the exit is visible and real, debt is a thing you walk through. When the exit is fuzzy, debt is a room you get stuck in.

## The Asymmetry You Want Them to Notice

Here's a truth worth sitting with: you can plan your way INTO debt in five minutes. A website, a quick application, an approved amount, and you've borrowed money before you've thought through much at all.

Planning your way OUT takes longer. Not forever — but it requires thought, calculation, commitment, and time. The entry is fast; the exit takes discipline.

That asymmetry is worth naming out loud with your teen. The reason people end up in debt they didn't fully intend to be in is that the door in is always easier than the door out. Financial systems are designed that way. It's not a conspiracy — it's just how lending works. Knowing this doesn't mean never borrowing. It means slowing down on the entry long enough to have a real exit planned before you walk through.

Pay a little thought now. Or a lot of money later.

## Try this together

Work through a hypothetical together — and keep it low-pressure and collaborative, not a test.

The scenario: your teen needs a $3,000 car to get to a job. They don't have $3,000 saved yet.

Talk through it together:

- **What's the smartest way in?** Could they save for three months first and reduce the loan? Is there a cheaper car that still works? What interest rate might they get, and does that make sense?
- **What's the exit plan?** If they borrowed $2,000 at a realistic rate for a young borrower, what's the monthly payment? When is it paid off? What's the total they'd pay including interest?
- **Is this good debt or a trap?** What makes it reasonable? What would make it unreasonable? (A car they can't afford the insurance on, a loan at a punishing interest rate, a car that costs more than the job pays, etc.)

Sketch it out on paper or in a notes app. This isn't a scary exercise — it's just thinking out loud about something they'll almost certainly face in the next few years. Walking through it now, when there's no pressure, builds the muscle for when the moment is real.

## Questions to sit with

**For yourself:**
- For each debt you're carrying right now, can you answer the exit question with real numbers — the payment, the payoff date, and the total you'll have paid including interest — or does any of it still come out vague?
- Looking honestly at your debts, which ones connect to something that earns income or holds value, and which are you still paying interest on long after the thing was used up?
- Where in your own life has the door into debt been faster than the door out — and what would slowing down the entry have changed if you'd planned the exit first?

**For you (the parent):**
- Do your current debts have clear exit plans? Do you know, specifically, when each one is paid off?
- What's one thing you've learned about debt that you wish you'd known earlier — and how do you pass that forward without making it a lecture?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- What would make a debt "worth it" — what conditions would have to be true for borrowing to make sense?
- What's the difference between a debt with an exit plan and one without one?
- If you were going to borrow money for something, what question would you make sure to answer before signing?
