**This week's big idea:** Getting a first job is a learnable, step-by-step process — and a teen who walks in with a simple résumé, a polite approach, and a little preparation will stand out from most of the competition.

## The Real Reason Teens Don't Apply

It's not laziness, usually. It's that the whole thing feels like a locked door with no key. Where do you even start? What do you put on a résumé when you've never had a job? Who do you even talk to when you walk in? What if they ask something you don't know how to answer?

These are real questions, and they're fixable. Your job this week isn't to push your kid into a job they're not ready for — it's to hand them the keys so the door doesn't feel locked anymore.

Even if your teen isn't applying tomorrow, having the tools ready changes their posture. It shifts them from "I can't do that yet" to "I know how to do that when I'm ready." That shift matters.

## Building a Résumé With No Experience

Here's the truth most teens don't hear: **no first-job hiring manager expects experience.** They know it's a first job. What they're looking for is someone who shows up on time, follows instructions, doesn't have a bad attitude, and can be trusted with basic tasks. A résumé is just a way of showing: I'm a real person who takes this seriously.

A solid first résumé has five parts:

1. **Name and contact info.** Full name, phone number, email address (make sure it's a professional one — not a handle from middle school). City and state is fine; no need for a full home address.

2. **A short objective or summary.** One or two sentences. Something like: *Dependable high school junior looking for a part-time position where I can contribute and learn. Available afternoons and weekends.* Simple. Honest. No fluff.

3. **Skills.** This is where teens underestimate themselves. Can they operate a cash register? Help customers? Organize shelves? Do they know basic computer skills? Are they good with younger kids? Speak a second language? Help with animals? Any of these counts.

4. **School information.** School name, current grade or expected graduation year, GPA if it's decent (3.0 and above is worth putting). Any honors, clubs, sports, or activities belong here too.

5. **Volunteer work, family responsibilities, or informal jobs.** This is gold that most teens ignore. Mowing the neighbor's lawn for three summers, helping at a school fundraiser, babysitting regularly, walking dogs in the neighborhood — all of it counts. Give it a title. "Babysitter, Jones Family, 2022–present" is legitimate work history.

One page. Clean font. No decorative graphics. You can find free templates at Canva, Google Docs, or Microsoft Word that look professional in about fifteen minutes.

## Where to Apply

For a first job, think local and think simple. Big employers with lots of turnover are often the most willing to hire someone without experience:

- Fast food and casual dining restaurants
- Grocery stores and retail chains
- Movie theaters
- Golf courses, parks, or recreation centers
- Lawn care or landscaping companies (seasonal)
- Pet stores or animal shelters
- Summer camps or youth programs
- Libraries (check local listings)

Beyond traditional jobs, **hustles count too.** Lawn mowing, babysitting, dog walking, tutoring younger kids, helping elderly neighbors with errands or tech — these build the same muscles as a formal job, and they're often easier to start. Some teens build real income this way before ever landing a W-2 job.

The goal isn't the perfect first job. The goal is the first job. Any of these will teach your kid more about money, responsibility, and follow-through than another summer of sleeping in.

## How to Walk In and Ask

This part intimidates most teens more than the résumé itself. Here's a simple script they can use:

Walk in when it's not peak busy (mid-morning on a weekday is usually good). Ask to speak to the manager. When the manager comes over, make eye contact, smile, and say:

*"Hi, my name is [Name]. I'm looking for a part-time position and I wanted to drop off my résumé and introduce myself. Is there anything available?"*

Then hand over the résumé, listen, and say thank you whether the answer is yes, no, or maybe. Before leaving: *"Is it okay if I follow up in a week?"* And then follow up in exactly one week.

That's it. That script, done with a calm voice and a real smile, will put your kid ahead of half the teens who submit an online form and never show their face.

## How to Ask an Adult for a Reference

References are something your kid will need at some point — and they're worth starting to think about now. A reference is simply an adult who can vouch for your character and work ethic. Teachers, coaches, neighbors, family friends, religious leaders, anyone who has seen your kid show up and do their best.

Teach your teen to ask directly and respectfully: *"I'm applying for a job and I was wondering if you'd be willing to be a reference for me. You could say no — I just wanted to ask."* Most adults are genuinely glad to help when asked that way. Then brief them — what job, what qualities matter, when they might be contacted.

And here's the important part your kid needs to hear: **the way you earn a great reference is by being the kind of person we talked about last week.** Show up on time. Do what you said. Be someone worth bragging about. The reference conversation isn't where the reference is won — it's just where it's collected.

## Try this together

This week, draft a real one-page résumé for your kid together. Even if they're not applying anywhere yet.

Start by asking them: "What have you done that someone paid you for, or that you did to help someone?" You'll probably get more than you expect once you start digging — the babysitting, the neighbors, the tasks around the house. Write it all down. Then add school info, skills, and a simple objective line.

Use a free template from Google Docs or Canva. Aim for clean, one page, their name at the top. Print it out, hold it, and let it feel real.

Then together, name three places they'd actually want to apply — restaurants, stores, community spots. Look up whether those places accept walk-ins or online applications. The goal is to have the résumé ready and three targets identified before the week ends.

Having a résumé in hand makes everything else feel possible. That's the win this week.

## Questions to sit with

**For yourself:**
- If you had to update your own résumé to one clean page today, what work, skills, and informal experience are you underselling or leaving off entirely?
- Where in your life are you stuck behind a "locked door with no key" — a job, a raise, an opportunity — because the steps feel unknown rather than actually impossible?
- Who are the people who could vouch for your character and work ethic right now, and are you the kind of person they'd be glad to put their own name behind?

**For you (the parent):**
- What did your first job teach you that you didn't expect — and have you shared that story with your kid?
- Is there anything you're unconsciously holding your kid back from because you worry they're not ready? What would "ready enough" actually look like?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- What three places would you actually want to work, and what is it about each one that sounds decent?
- What's one thing you've done — paid or unpaid — that shows you can be trusted with a responsibility?
- If a manager asked you "why should I hire you instead of someone else?" what would you say?
