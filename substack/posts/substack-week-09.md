**This week's big idea:** Save a slice off the top, before anything else gets a chance to eat it. Not what's left at the end — the first piece, automatically, every time. That one shift changes everything.

## Why "Save What's Left" Always Fails

Here's the thing about saving what's left: there's never anything left.

You probably already know this from your own life. Money lands in the account, and by the time the groceries, the gas, the random Tuesday thing, and the whatever-that-was charge clear, the "leftover" is either gone or embarrassingly small. And your teen is going to experience exactly the same thing — on a smaller scale, maybe, but the pattern is identical.

The reason isn't lack of discipline. It's not a character flaw. It's just the math of how money flows when it doesn't have a job waiting for it the moment it arrives. Spending is passive. It just happens. Saving is active — it requires a decision every single time. And most of us lose that battle most of the time, because we're tired and we have other things to think about.

The fix is to make saving passive and spending what's active. You flip the order.

Pay yourself first means that before rent, before gas, before anything — a piece of every dollar that comes in goes to your future. Not because you're especially disciplined. Because you set it up that way and it happens automatically.

## The Muscle Under the Habit

Here's why this matters even more for your teen than for you: it builds an identity.

When your kid moves $20 to savings the same day their paycheck or allowance lands, every single time, they stop being "someone who's trying to save" and become "someone who saves." That sounds small. It's not. Identity is the stickiest thing there is. A kid who sees themselves as a saver behaves like a saver — consistently, without as much friction — in a way that no amount of willpower can manufacture.

Think of it like a muscle. The first few reps feel weird. After a few weeks, it's just what you do. And after a few months? Skipping the transfer is the thing that feels off.

This is also where compounding starts. Not just the compounding of money sitting in a savings account, but the compounding of the habit itself. Small consistent choices don't just add up — they multiply. Six months of paying yourself first builds the groundwork that the bigger money decisions later in life will be stacked on.

**Future You is built in increments.** Every transfer, however small, is your kid making a real deposit into the life they're building. That's not a metaphor. It's the actual mechanism.

## The Percentage That Starts Everything

You do not need a big number to start. You need a consistent number.

Ten percent is the classic starting point. It's round, it's manageable, and it's been used as a savings target across so many different traditions and time periods for a reason — it works without hurting. On a $50 allowance, that's $5. On a $200 first paycheck, that's $20. Not life-changing on its own. Life-changing over time.

If ten percent feels like too much right now, five percent is fine. The number matters less than the act. The goal this week isn't to optimize the percentage — it's to build the reflex.

What kills this habit is flexibility. "I'll move it when I figure out what I need this week" is how the habit never forms. The transfer has to happen the same day the money arrives. That's the non-negotiable part. Set a reminder. Make it a rule. Create the groove.

If your teen is old enough to have a bank account with direct deposit or an easy transfer option, setting up an automatic transfer is even better. Automation removes the decision entirely — and that means the habit doesn't depend on motivation, which is famously unreliable.

## What to Do With What's Left

Once that off-the-top slice is set aside, your teen gets to spend the rest. All of it, if they want. This isn't about deprivation — it's about protecting the future first.

This matters for how you frame it. "Pay yourself first" sounds good. "You only get to spend 90% of what you earn" sounds like punishment. Same math, totally different energy.

The way to talk about it: the first piece is already gone — it belongs to Future You. Everything else is yours, and you get to decide what to do with it. That framing puts the spending money back in their control rather than making them feel like they're losing something.

Some teens will immediately want to know what the savings pile is FOR. That's a great conversation — and if you don't have one yet, "it's for Future You, who will definitely have something better to use it for than you can think of right now" is a perfectly honest answer. You don't need a goal to start saving. You just need to start.

## Automate It, Then Forget It

The most powerful version of this habit isn't willpower-dependent at all. It's automatic.

Once your teen has a savings account (which we'll tackle next week), the ideal setup is: money arrives, a fixed amount moves to savings immediately, the rest is available for life. Repeat every time.

For a teen who gets paid irregularly — babysitting, mowing lawns, selling things online — it can't be fully automated. So the rule becomes: the day money comes in, transfer the percentage before doing anything else. Before checking prices on anything. Before deciding what you need. First.

You might need to help make this happen for younger teens. Walk them through the transfer. Sit with them the day of the paycheck. Make it a 90-second ritual you do together until it sticks on its own. The goal is that eventually it happens without you — but there's no shame in scaffolding it at the start.

## Try this together

This week, agree on a "pay yourself first" percentage with your teen — ten percent is the target, but five percent works if it's a stretch. Write it down together.

Then wait for money. The next dollar that comes in — allowance, cash for a birthday, pay from a job — move that percentage immediately, that same day. Do it together if you can. If they have a savings account, transfer it there. If not, put it physically in an envelope labeled "Future [Their Name]" and set it somewhere visible.

Talk through it while you do it: "This piece is done. It already went where it needed to go. Everything else is yours."

If your teen is skeptical or eye-roll-y, acknowledge that it feels abstract. That's fair. Tell them the habit is the whole point right now — not the amount. They're building something that will matter a lot more in five years than it does today.

## Questions to sit with

**For yourself:**
- When money lands in your account, what happens first — a transfer to your future, or the slow leak of whatever-comes-up? Be honest about which one is actually running.
- What percentage off the top could you move automatically this week without feeling the pinch — five, ten, more? What's stopped you from setting it up before now?
- If you'd paid yourself first, automatically, for the last five years, where would you be standing today — and what does that tell you about the next five?

**For you (the parent):**
- Do you pay yourself first — automatically, before anything else? If not, what would it take to start?
- What money habit do you wish you'd built at your kid's age? Is there a way to tell that story without the lecture?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- What percentage off the top feels doable — even if it's small? What would ten percent of your last paycheck or allowance have been?
- If you saved that percentage every time money came in for the next year, what do you think you'd have by now?
- What feels hardest about moving money to savings before you've touched the rest?
