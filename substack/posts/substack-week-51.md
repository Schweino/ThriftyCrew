**This week's big idea:** Big dreams are great — your kid needs them. But the work always lives in the small daily steps. A one-page five-year plan connects the dream at the top to the tiny first move at the bottom, and that connection is what makes real progress possible.

## Why Plans Feel Intimidating (and Why They Shouldn't)

When most people hear "five-year plan," they picture a formal document — polished, detailed, probably somehow wrong by next Thursday. That version is intimidating, and it should be, because it's not what we're building.

What we're building is much simpler. It's a one-page document with a big dream at the top and a small first step at the bottom. It doesn't have to be perfect. It doesn't have to be final. It just has to be specific enough to point in a direction — and honest enough to name what the next tiny move looks like.

For a teenager, this is genuinely powerful. Most teens operate on a pretty short time horizon by default. The future is abstract. "Future You" (remember Week 1?) can feel like a cartoon character with no connection to the person standing in front of you. A written plan makes the connection visible. It turns a vague wish into an actual intention — and intentions are much easier to act on.

You're not locking in a career path or committing to a university or promising a specific salary. You're just answering: what direction, roughly? And what's one thing I can do about it this week?

## Dream Big at the Top — Then Work Down

Here's the structure that works. It has six layers, and you move from the big to the small:

**1. The Big Dream**
This is allowed to be ambitious. If your kid could fast-forward five years and peek at the life they'd love to be living, what do they see? Where are they? What are they doing? What does their financial situation look like, roughly? Don't edit this down to what seems "realistic." Let it be real first.

**2. The Direction**
This is the general path. Not a specific job title, but a direction: building something, working with people, working with their hands, creating, solving problems, going to college, learning a trade, starting a side business. The direction doesn't have to be perfectly clear — fuzzy is fine. The question is just: which general way am I facing?

**3. This Year's Goals**
Three or four specific things for the next 12 months. Not dreams — goals. Things that can be checked off. "Get a part-time job." "Save $X." "Research two schools/programs I'm interested in." "Learn one real skill." These should feel achievable but require effort.

**4. Tiny First Steps**
This is the most important layer. For each of this year's goals, what's the smallest possible action that moves toward it? The tiniest first step. Not "figure out my whole career" — "look up two programs online for 20 minutes on Sunday." Not "save $1,000" — "open the savings account and move $20 this week." Tiny. Doable. Right now.

**5. Who to Ask for Help**
A plan built in isolation is a weaker plan. Who does your kid know (or who could they meet) who's already living something close to this direction? One or two names. What's one question they could ask? This is borrowed hindsight in action — skip the years of trial and error by talking to someone who's already done it.

**6. How You'll Celebrate Wins**
Because this entire year has been about counting victories, not missing checkpoints. Pick two or three milestones in the year ahead and name how you'll celebrate when you hit them. Small wins deserve acknowledgment. That acknowledgment is the fuel.

## The Plan Isn't the Point — The Habit Is

Here's something worth telling your teen: the act of making a plan matters more than having the perfect plan.

Plans change. Directions shift. The thing your kid is excited about today might not be what grabs them in three years, and that's completely normal. This is not a binding contract with the future. It's a current-best-answer, made with the information available right now.

What the plan does is create a reference point. When choices come up — which class to take, whether to apply for a job, how to spend the summer — there's something to check it against. "Does this move me toward my direction, or away from it?" That one question, consulted regularly, cuts through a lot of noise.

And revisiting the plan — even once a year — is itself a valuable habit. People who write down goals and check them periodically don't achieve everything they write. But they achieve far more than people who don't write anything down at all.

## Don't Plan Alone

If there's one thing to repeat from the last several weeks, it's this: your kid doesn't have to figure this out from scratch.

Someone is already living something close to what they're aiming for. Someone has already made the mistakes your kid is about to make, already picked up the skills they'll need, already learned which paths are dead ends. Asking that person — one hour over coffee or a message online — is worth months of solo trial and error.

Help your kid put one real name on the plan. Not a celebrity or a vague idea, but an actual person: a relative, a friend's parent, someone from a mentor program, a former coach, a teacher. One person whose life or career even roughly points in the same direction. And help them draft one honest question to ask.

That conversation might not happen immediately. But putting the name on the plan means it becomes a real intention rather than a "someday I should probably talk to someone" that never happens.

## A Note for You, the Parent

It's worth mentioning: your kid watching you fill out this plan alongside them is more powerful than any lecture about goal-setting could ever be. If you have a five-year plan — even a rough one, even an uncertain one — share it. If you don't, this is a good week to sketch one out.

You don't need to perform certainty you don't have. "I'm not totally sure, but the direction I'm heading is..." is a completely honest and useful thing to say. Modeling that plans can be imperfect and still useful is one of the most helpful things you can show.

## Try this together

Sit down together this week and fill out the template above — one for your kid, and ideally one for yourself too. This doesn't need to be a long session. An hour is plenty. The goal isn't a polished document; it's a real conversation that results in something written down.

A few tips for the session: start with the Big Dream before touching anything else. Let it be big. Resist the urge to immediately problem-solve or reality-check it. The editing comes later; the dreaming comes first. Then work your way down to the tiny first steps, which is where most of the session's energy should land — because those are the ones that actually happen.

When you're done, put the plan somewhere visible. Fridge, bedroom door, a folder they'll actually open. Not hidden in a notebook. And set a specific date to check in on it — ideally six months from now.

## Questions to sit with

**For yourself:**
- If you fast-forward five years to the life you'd love to be living, what does it actually look like — and have you ever let yourself name the big dream without editing it down to "realistic"?
- For the direction you're heading, what's one tiny first step — something you could do in the next 20 minutes this week — that you keep putting off?
- Who do you know who's already living something close to where you want to go, and what's one honest question you could ask them?

**For you (the parent):**
- What is your own five-year direction, roughly? Have you written it down? If not, this is the week.
- What would you have done differently at your kid's age if someone had sat down and helped you make a plan like this?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- What's the big dream — not the "realistic" version, the real one?
- If you had to name the very first tiny step you could take this week toward one of your goals, what would it be?
- Who's one person living something close to what you want? What would you ask them?
- How will you know this year has been a good one? What would make you proud to look back on?
