# Week 5 — Needs First, Then Wants

*One simple rule — protect what you need before you plan what you want — prevents most of the money stress adults carry for decades.*

**This week's big idea:** Your kid's brain will quietly dress up wants as needs every single day. Teaching them to see through that costume — and to plan fun on purpose rather than spending on impulse — is one of the most practical money skills you can hand them.

## The Brain Is Very Good at Lying to You

Here's something nobody likes to admit: most of us don't really know the difference between a need and a want in the moment. We feel it, so it must be real. "I need new shoes." "I need to go out with my friends this weekend." "I need this app."

That feeling isn't wrong, exactly. It's just the brain doing what it always does — building a case for what it already wants.

A **need** is something that keeps your life functional and your obligations covered. Food. A place to sleep. Transportation to work or school. The phone bill, if that's your lifeline. A **want** is everything else — even the things that feel urgent, that everyone else seems to have, that you've been thinking about for weeks.

The line between the two is rarely as clear as a textbook makes it sound. But here's a practical test your kid can actually use: "If I skip this, does my life fall apart — or just get less fun?" If the answer is "less fun," that's a want. And there's nothing wrong with wants. The problem is when we fund wants before needs are covered, or when we spend on wants impulsively instead of planning them.

## Why This Rule Is Worth Memorizing

Think about the financial stress most adults carry. Go underneath it and you'll usually find the same thing: money that was supposed to cover something important got spent on something less important first. The rent that got tight because spending happened before the bills did. The savings account that never grew because fun came off the top.

This isn't a character flaw. It's what happens when nobody ever said the rule out loud.

The rule is simple: **needs first, then wants.** Cover your obligations, set aside something for the future, and then — only then — plan your fun. This isn't deprivation. It's sequence. And the sequence changes everything.

Your kid won't always like it. They'll push back, especially when "I need those shoes for the team" feels completely true to them. That's normal. The skill isn't to never want things. It's to notice the want for what it is, and put it in the right order.

## The Word "Need" Is Working Hard and Getting Paid Too Much

Walk your kid through a few examples this week. Ask them: Is this a need or a want?

- A winter coat when theirs is destroyed — need.
- The *specific brand* of winter coat their friends have — want.
- Lunch — need.
- Lunch at the new place that's a little more expensive — want.
- Getting to school — need.
- Having the newest phone to do it — want.

The game isn't to say wants are bad. The game is to call them what they are. When your teen can name a want without shame — "yeah, that's a want, but I really do want it" — they're in a completely different position than a kid who has to convince themselves it's a need to justify the purchase.

That clarity is the tool. Once they can see it clearly, they can plan for it honestly.

## Plan Fun on Purpose

Here's the flip side of "needs first" that often gets missed: once needs are covered, **wants get to happen on purpose.**

This is important. If you only tell your kid what they can't do, you've given them half the lesson. The other half is showing them that planning for a want — actually saving for it, putting a date on it, looking forward to it — is more satisfying than impulse-buying it.

There's real evidence for this. The anticipation of a planned treat often delivers more happiness than the thing itself. And the thing itself feels better when you saved for it, because you're not carrying the quiet stress of wondering whether you should have spent that money.

Help your teen pick one want they've been circling — a piece of gear, a concert, a night out with friends, new clothes — and make it a planned goal instead of a guilty impulse. Put a number on it. Put a timeline on it. That's not withholding. That's giving them the version of the thing that actually feels good.

## The Long Game Behind a Simple Rule

**Future You** benefits enormously from this habit. Every time your kid lets a need get funded before a want, they're building the muscle that will protect them from some of the most common financial disasters adults face: overdrafts because fun money hit before the electric bill did, credit card debt because wants kept happening before savings did, stress that slowly builds from always being slightly behind.

The habit doesn't have to be rigid. Life is messy, and sometimes the plan needs to flex. But the sequence — needs, then savings, then fun — gives every dollar a clear role. And a dollar with a job doesn't wander.

Start small. This week, just build the awareness. No shame, no lectures. Just learning to name the difference, and starting to see the costume the brain puts on its wants.

## Try this together

Pull up your kid's last 10 purchases — check their bank app, Venmo history, or just ask them to recall. Write them down on a piece of paper or in the Notes app.

Now, together, sort each one into two columns: **NEED** or **WANT.**

A few ground rules for making this work:
1. No shame. This is a fact-finding mission, not a verdict.
2. You sort your own recent purchases alongside theirs — this works better when you're doing it together, not administering a quiz.
3. If something is genuinely in between ("I needed shoes, but these were the expensive ones"), split it: the basic need was real, the upgrade was a want.

When you're done, just look at the numbers. What percentage was needs? What percentage was wants? Was anything surprising?

That's it. No lecture needed. The awareness itself does the work.

If you want to take it one step further: pick one want from the list and figure out what a plan would have looked like. How many weeks of saving? Was it worth it in hindsight?

## Questions to sit with

**For you (the parent):**
- Where in your own spending do you treat a want like a need — and has naming it changed anything?
- How do you sequence your own spending? Needs first, or does fun sometimes sneak to the front?
- What one want are you planning on purpose, rather than spending on impulse?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- What's one thing you've been calling a need that's actually a want? (No wrong answers — just honest ones.)
- If you planned for one want right now — picked a goal and a timeline — what would it be?
- When you look at the list from our exercise, were you surprised by anything?
