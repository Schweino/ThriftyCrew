# Week 31 — The 401(k) and "Free Money"

*Most adults leave part of their salary on the table every year without realizing it. Your kid doesn't have to.*

**This week's big idea:** When your teen eventually lands a job with benefits, an employer 401(k) match is one of the best financial deals they'll ever be offered — free money added to their retirement savings just for participating. Plant the seed now so they grab it automatically.

---

*This lesson is educational, not financial advice. 401(k) rules, contribution limits, and employer policies vary. Encourage your teen (and yourself) to consult a financial professional when making real decisions about retirement accounts.*

---

## The Deal Most People Don't Know They're Turning Down

Here's a scenario: your kid starts their first real full-time job at 22. HR hands them a stack of paperwork, walks them through insurance options, and somewhere near the bottom mentions a 401(k). Your kid — overwhelmed, maybe nervous, definitely not sleeping enough — checks "no thanks" or leaves it blank. They figure they'll deal with it later.

What they don't realize: they just turned down a raise.

Many employers offer what's called a **match** — they agree to contribute money to your 401(k) based on how much you contribute. A common structure is something like: "We'll match 50% of what you put in, up to 6% of your salary." 

That sounds technical, so let's make it concrete. If your kid earns $40,000 and contributes 6% ($2,400/year), the employer adds 50% of that — another $1,200 — at no cost. For doing nothing except checking a box.

The employer match is, without exaggeration, free money. It is the closest thing to an instant 50% or 100% return that exists in legitimate personal finance. And yet a surprising number of employees — especially younger ones who are focused on rent and car payments — either don't enroll or don't contribute enough to capture the full match.

Your kid doesn't have to be one of them.

## What a 401(k) Is, in Plain Terms

A **401(k)** is a retirement savings account offered through an employer. The name comes from the tax code that created it, which is not important. What is important:

Money goes into the account from each paycheck, usually before taxes are taken out. This means your kid is contributing money they haven't paid income tax on yet — which lowers their taxable income today. The money grows in the account (invested in funds, often something like the index funds we covered last week) and ideally isn't touched until retirement. At that point, they pay taxes on withdrawals.

There are contribution limits set by the IRS each year. For 2024, the limit is $23,000 for most people under 50. Your teen won't be hitting that ceiling in their first job, but the principle is: contribute at minimum enough to get the full employer match. That's the baseline. Everything else is a bonus.

There are also Roth 401(k) options at some employers, which work differently (contributions are after-tax, but growth and withdrawals in retirement are tax-free). Your teen may encounter this choice someday — for now, the important thing is knowing the basic structure, not memorizing every variation.

## Why Planting This Seed Now Matters

Your kid is probably not 22 yet. They may be 13, 15, 17. So why are we talking about this now?

Because the moment of decision — that HR paperwork moment — happens fast. It happens when a kid is excited about their new job, overwhelmed by adult responsibilities, and probably not thinking about money they won't see for 40 years.

The teens who grab the match are almost always the ones who heard about it first. Who had a parent say, at some point before the job started: "When you start a real job and they offer a 401(k), get at least enough in to capture the full match. Whatever they'll match, you give them that, minimum."

That sentence — delivered calmly, memorably, without a lecture — is what this week is about. You're not teaching your teen to be a financial expert. You're planting one clear rule so that when the moment comes, the answer is automatic.

## The Time Variable (Again)

We've talked about how time is the superpower in compounding. The 401(k) makes this even more dramatic.

Consider two people. One starts contributing to a 401(k) at 22 and never increases her contributions beyond what captures the full match. The other waits until 32 to start. By retirement, the early starter — despite contributing the same annual amount — can end up with dramatically more money, simply because she started 10 years sooner. That extra decade of compound growth does a lot of heavy lifting.

Your kid starting at 22 instead of 32 is not a small edge. Over a 40-year working life, it can mean the difference between financial comfort and financial stress in retirement. And they don't have to sacrifice anything to get it — just start early, grab the match, and let time work.

This is worth saying out loud to your teen. Not as a lecture. More like: "The people who retire comfortably aren't usually the ones who made the most money — they're the ones who started earliest and grabbed every match their employer offered."

## If You Have a 401(k) — Use It

If you have a 401(k) at your own job, this week is a chance to make it tangible. Pull it up. Show your teen — not the balance necessarily, but the concept. Show them the match. Show them the fund it's invested in (this is a great moment to connect to last week's index fund conversation — many 401(k) options include low-cost index funds).

If you're not currently contributing enough to capture your full employer match, this lesson is also for you. It is, genuinely, free money. Whatever the reason you've put it off, this week is as good a time as any to change that.

And if you don't have a 401(k) — maybe you're self-employed, or between jobs, or haven't been offered one — that's okay. You can still teach the concept with a hypothetical example. "If I earned $50,000 and my employer would match up to 3% — $1,500 — what should I do?" Your teen can work through the math themselves.

## Try this together

This week, make the 401(k) match feel real with a concrete example.

Start with this math: Pick a hypothetical first salary — maybe $35,000 or $40,000. Then imagine an employer who matches 50% of contributions up to 6% of salary. Work out the numbers together:
- How much would your kid need to contribute to get the full match?
- How much would the employer add?
- What's the total going into the account just in year one?

Then stretch it: what if that total — their contribution plus the match — grew for 40 years in an index fund averaging a modest 6% annually? Use a free compound interest calculator online to see the final number. (A quick note: past returns don't guarantee future results — this is illustrative.)

If you have a 401(k) of your own, spend 15 minutes looking at it together. Are you getting the full match? What's the money invested in?

Let the conversation be simple. The goal isn't to teach your kid everything about retirement planning. The goal is to make "always grab the match" feel like an obvious, obvious rule — so obvious that they'll do it without thinking.

## Questions to sit with

**For you (the parent):**
- Are you currently capturing your full employer match, if you have one? If not, what's standing in the way?
- Have you ever had a job with a match and not maximized it? What did that cost you — roughly?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- If your future boss offered to add 50 cents for every dollar you saved, up to a limit — what would you do?
- What's the one rule you'll remember when you start your first job with benefits?
- Why do you think some people still don't enroll, even when there's free money on the table?
