# Week 46 — How to Spot Good Advice From Bad

*Your kid is swimming in advice. The problem isn't too little of it — it's that a lot of it comes from people who've never been where your kid is trying to go.*

**This week's big idea:** There's a simple filter for separating advice worth following from advice that'll cost your kid time, money, and momentum: look at the life of the person giving it. Don't take directions to a place from someone who's never been there.

## The Advice Flood Is Real

Your kid is getting more advice than any generation before them — from teachers, parents, coaches, relatives, older siblings, friends, YouTube channels, TikTok creators, podcasters, Reddit threads, and the relentless chorus of opinion that lives in their pocket twenty-four hours a day.

Almost none of those sources are labeled. There's no badge that says "this person knows what they're talking about" or "this person is guessing, just like you are."

So your kid does what all of us do: they pick up on confidence. The person who sounds certain must know. The person with a lot of followers must be right. The relative who gives advice loudly at every family gathering must have earned that volume somehow.

Except that's not how it works. Confidence and certainty are not evidence of results. And this week, your job is to give your kid a better filter than "who sounds the most sure."

## The One Question That Cuts Through Everything

The filter is this: **look at the life, not just the words.**

Ask one question about any source of advice — whether it's a person in real life or someone your kid follows online:

*Would I want their actual life?*

Not their highlight reel. Not the version of their life they talk about on video or at the dinner table. Their actual life — the relationships, the finances, the day-to-day, the stress level, the choices that got them here.

If the answer is yes, their advice is worth taking seriously. If the answer is no — or "I don't know because I've never actually looked" — then treat that advice with real caution, no matter how confident it sounds.

This isn't about disrespecting people. It's about applying the same logic you'd use anywhere else. If you wanted directions to a city you'd never visited, you'd ask someone who'd been there — not someone who'd heard about it once. Advice about money, career, and life works the same way.

## The Problem With "Sounding Right"

Here's what makes this tricky for teenagers: the people giving the worst advice often sound the most certain.

Someone who made every financial mistake in the book doesn't always say "don't do what I did." Often they redecorate their mistakes as wisdom — and they say it with total conviction. A person who's never built savings will tell your kid exactly how savings work. A person who's never held a job they loved will explain passionately why following your passion is a trap. A person whose business failed will sometimes tell your kid not to start one.

The advice isn't useless just because their outcomes were hard. But it's worth knowing the track record. It's worth asking: "What did this person actually build, and is it something I'd want?"

Meanwhile, the people with the most useful hindsight are often the quietest about it. They're not performing expertise — they're just living it. They're the ones your kid might have to go find intentionally, like we talked about last week.

## Who to Actually Listen To

A good rule of thumb: find the person living closest to the life your kid is aiming for, and ask them the most direct questions you can.

That doesn't mean finding someone famous or extraordinarily successful. It means finding someone who is a few chapters ahead — someone who made the financial choices that left them with breathing room, built a career in a field your kid is genuinely curious about, or navigated the decision your kid is facing right now.

The closer their situation is to what your kid is trying to accomplish, the more directly their experience translates. A fifty-year-old who built a career in medicine in a different era may have less immediately useful advice for a kid trying to break into graphic design than a twenty-six-year-old who did it two years ago.

Both can be worth listening to. But calibrate the weight accordingly. Life advice, like any other direction, is most useful from someone who just made the drive.

## Online Advice and the Confidence Trap

This one deserves its own moment, because the internet has supercharged the advice problem.

Online creators have every incentive to sound authoritative. Confidence gets clicks. Certainty gets followers. Hedging and nuance are bad for engagement. So the most confident voices online are often the ones with the worst incentives to be honest — because their income depends on sounding like they know the answer, not on you actually getting good results.

This doesn't mean everything online is bad. There are genuinely useful voices, people who share real experience and explain real things in plain terms. But the filter still applies: what has this person actually built? Are they showing you their actual results, or just their posture?

When your kid is watching someone online explain how money works, or which career to pursue, or how to build a business — a few good questions are worth asking:

- Has this person actually done what they're teaching?
- What's their evidence? Not just their confidence — their evidence?
- Are they selling something? (Not disqualifying, but worth knowing.)
- Would I want the life behind the camera, not just the content?

These questions slow things down just enough to let common sense catch up.

## Try this together

This week, pick two sources of advice that are already in your kid's life — one person they know in real life, one voice they follow online. These could be a relative, a coach, a YouTube channel, a podcast host, a social media creator — whatever's real for your kid.

For each one, walk through these questions together:

1. What do we actually know about their life — not what they present, but their actual situation?
2. Would your kid genuinely want their real life, not just the version they share?
3. What specifically qualifies them to give advice on this topic?
4. If there are gaps in what you know, what would you want to find out before trusting their advice?

Keep the tone light and curious — this isn't a criticism session. Frame it as building a personal filter, not an attack on any individual. The goal is to help your kid develop the habit of asking these questions automatically, without your prompting, every time advice arrives.

If it helps to anchor the conversation: think together about a time advice was wrong — when someone told your kid (or you) to do something that didn't pan out. What was it about that person or that situation that made the advice less reliable than it seemed?

## Questions to sit with

**For you (the parent):**
- Whose advice do you actually trust, and if you had to articulate why, what would you say?
- Have you ever passed on advice to your kid that you picked up from someone whose life didn't really back it up? (No shame — most of us have.)
- What have you done to help your kid identify trustworthy adult voices outside the family?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- Who are the top two or three people — in your life or online — whose advice you take most seriously? Would you want their actual life, not just the parts they show?
- Has anyone ever given you advice that turned out to be wrong, or led you somewhere you didn't want to go? Looking back, what was off about that source?
- When you're trying to figure something out, where's the first place you go — and is that a good place to go?
