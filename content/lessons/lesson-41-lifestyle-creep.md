# Week 41 — Lifestyle Creep

*A raise is a win — unless you spend it. Here's how to teach your kid to keep winning.*

**This week's big idea:** When income goes up and spending goes up to match, you end up in exactly the same place — just with a higher monthly burn. The gap doesn't grow. The wealth doesn't build. Teaching your kid to resist lifestyle inflation before they ever get their first raise is one of the most valuable things you can do for their future.

## The Raise That Disappears

There's a pattern that plays out so predictably it's almost a law of nature. A person earns more money. They feel like they "finally" can afford the nicer apartment, the newer phone, the better car payment, the subscription services they've been eyeing. Each upgrade feels earned and reasonable. And then they look up a year later and realize they're still living paycheck to paycheck — just with nicer stuff.

This is **lifestyle creep**. Income rises. Spending rises to fill it. The gap — money in minus money out — stays exactly the same. All that extra earning goes straight into maintaining a bigger life rather than building anything.

The thing that makes this so sneaky is that each individual upgrade seems perfectly justified. Of course you deserve a better apartment once you can afford it. Of course you can treat yourself when you got a promotion. But when *every* raise triggers a round of upgrades, none of the extra income ever compounds into anything real.

## Why It Starts Young

Your kid might not be earning a salary yet, but they're already getting a version of this lesson — they just don't know it.

Think about the moment their allowance went up, or they got a bigger birthday haul than usual, or they picked up their first job. What happened to that extra money? For most kids, it evaporated into more snacks, more gaming, more stuff — things that felt fine in the moment but didn't build anything. That's lifestyle creep in its training-wheels form.

The reason to talk about this now, before the bigger paychecks arrive, is that the habits form first. A kid who gets used to keeping a steady percentage of whatever comes in — regardless of the total — will carry that reflex into adulthood. A kid who learns to spend every new dollar will carry *that* habit into adulthood instead.

You're not trying to raise a miser. You're trying to raise someone who notices when their lifestyle is inflating, and who makes that choice on purpose instead of by accident.

## The Mental Trick That Helps

Here's one reframe that actually works: **treat every raise as if half of it doesn't exist.**

When income goes up, it's easy to update your mental picture of "what I earn" to the new, higher number. You feel wealthier, so you spend like it. The trick is to hold onto the old number for your baseline lifestyle — and quietly funnel the difference somewhere that builds.

For a teen, this might look like: "My hours went from 10 to 15 a week — I'm going to live like I'm still working 10, and let the extra go toward savings or my investment account." For an adult, it looks like: salary goes up by $5,000 and the lifestyle stays the same, so $5,000 actually compounds.

This isn't deprivation. You can still upgrade things — intentionally, after deciding it's the best use of that money. The upgrade just shouldn't happen automatically because there was money left over.

## The Freedom You're Actually Buying

Here's the thing your kid probably hasn't thought about yet: the person who keeps their lifestyle modest while their income grows is buying something that can't be advertised or photographed for a social feed. They're buying **options**.

When you don't need every dollar to cover your life, you get to make choices. You can take a risk on a job you love instead of a job that just pays. You can weather a tough month without it being a crisis. You can say no to something that doesn't feel right, because you're not financially cornered. That's a form of freedom that the person who upgraded everything can't afford — no matter what they earn.

This is what a **wide gap** actually buys: not a bigger number in an account, but the ability to live on your own terms. And the earlier a kid understands that, the more of it they can build.

## Lifestyle Creep Is Not a Moral Failure

One more thing worth saying out loud: enjoying a better quality of life as you earn more is not wrong. If your kid can genuinely afford a nicer phone and it makes them happy, that's not a problem. The problem is the automatic, unexamined version — where every bit of extra income just vanishes into a slightly puffier life before anyone decided that's what they wanted.

The goal is intentionality. Spend the raise if you want — but spend it because you chose to, not because you didn't notice it happening. And if you don't spend it? Future You is quietly stacking.

## Try this together

Think back to a time when your household had more money coming in — a bonus, a raise, a year where things went well. Be honest with yourself about what happened to that extra money. Did the lifestyle inflate to meet it? Did it disappear into restaurant meals, subscriptions, or upgrades that you barely remember now?

Share that with your kid this week. Not as a regret, just as a real example of how it works. Then ask them: "If you doubled what you're earning right now — from babysitting, your job, whatever — what would you do with the extra?"

Listen to their answer. If they say they'd spend it, don't lecture. Just ask: "What would the version of you who kept the lifestyle steady do instead?" Let them think about it. Then both of you write down one number — the percentage of any new money you want to keep instead of spend. Put it somewhere visible.

If your kid currently earns anything, look at what's actually happening to it. Is their spending inflating with their income, or is there a gap? Don't make it a critique — just make it visible. "Here's what I notice. What do you notice?"

## Questions to sit with

**For you (the parent):**
- When has your own lifestyle inflated to match a raise or windfall, and what would you have differently with that extra money?
- Does your kid currently see you making intentional choices about raises and extra income, or does spending just automatically expand?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- If you got a raise at your job tomorrow, what would you actually do with the extra money?
- What's something you used to think you *needed* that you now barely think about — and what does that tell you about upgrading things?
- What would the gap look like if your income went up but your spending stayed the same for a year?
