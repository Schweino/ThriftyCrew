# Week 25 — References Are Currency

*The most valuable thing your kid takes from their first job isn't the paycheck — it's the people who'll vouch for them.*

**This week's big idea:** References aren't just a box to check on a job application. They're living proof of your kid's character, built one interaction at a time — and they compound in value the longer the relationship lasts.

## The Asset Nobody Talks About

We spend a lot of time teaching teens how to earn money. We talk about budgets, savings percentages, and first jobs. But there's a currency that doesn't show up in any bank account — and it might be the most important thing your kid builds in their teens and twenties.

It's the people who will pick up the phone and say, "Yes, hire that kid. I'd hire them again myself."

A good reference isn't just a name and number. It's a person who has watched your kid show up, do the work, and treat people well — and who is now willing to put their own reputation behind your kid's. That's enormous. That's the kind of backing that opens doors a résumé alone can't.

Here's the quiet part: most teens (and honestly, most adults) never think about this until they urgently need a reference, scrambling to remember if they left that job on good terms two years ago. Your kid can do this differently. They can think about it now, when there's time to actually build something.

## How References Are Built (It Isn't What You Think)

References aren't given. They're earned, and they're earned in the small moments that don't feel important at the time.

The boss who will rave about your kid is thinking about the Tuesday when it was slow and your kid cleaned the back storage room without being asked. The coach who'll write a glowing letter is remembering the practice your kid showed up to when everyone else was home sick. The teacher who will make a call on your kid's behalf is thinking about the semester your kid asked a good question and came in during office hours.

None of those moments felt significant. None of them were. But they stacked. And now they're stored in someone's memory as evidence that your kid is the real deal.

This is the reputation principle from a few weeks ago (Week 20) showing up in a very specific, practical form. Your kid's daily behavior becomes the source material for what others will say about them when they're not in the room.

The good news: this means any teen can build strong references, regardless of grades, connections, or experience. It's just behavior, repeated.

## The Art of Leaving Well

Here's a piece of hindsight that's worth borrowing: the way you leave a place matters as much as how you showed up.

Most teens (and plenty of adults) don't think about their exit until they're already out the door. They stop caring once they've decided to leave. They put in less effort their last two weeks. They don't say a real goodbye. They just vanish.

That's the moment a potential reference quietly decides they can't be enthusiastic.

Leaving well looks like this: give proper notice, work just as hard in your last week as your first, thank the people who taught you something, and tell your manager that you appreciated the opportunity. That's it. It takes about five minutes and it cements the relationship in a way that can benefit your kid for years.

A boss who watched your kid give their best on the last shift — even when they didn't have to — will remember that. They will tell the next person who calls exactly that.

Every role your kid leaves is a door that either stays open or quietly closes. Help them think about which it'll be before they decide it doesn't matter.

## Staying in Touch (Without It Being Weird)

References go cold if you never water them. Most people only reach out to former bosses and mentors when they need something — and that feels transactional, because it is. The fix is simpler than it sounds.

It's an email every six months. A quick note when something good happens: "Hey, just wanted to let you know I got into the program we talked about. Thank you for your encouragement." A check-in when they're in the area. Congratulating someone on a LinkedIn promotion.

These take three minutes. They keep the relationship alive. And they mean that when your kid eventually does need to ask for a reference, it doesn't feel like showing up empty-handed asking for a favor. It feels like catching up with someone who has been quietly invested in your kid's path.

Teach your kid this habit early and it will pay dividends they can't even see yet. Adults who genuinely care about your kid's future don't disappear — they just need to know you're still out there, still working hard, still someone they'd be proud to champion.

## How to Actually Ask for a Reference

This part makes most teens deeply uncomfortable, so let's make it concrete.

Asking for a reference is not presumptuous. It's appropriate. But there's a right way to do it.

First, choose carefully. Ask someone who has actually seen your kid work — not just someone they like. A family friend who barely knows them does your kid no favors compared to a manager who watched them handle a tough situation with grace.

Second, ask before you need one. Don't call someone an hour before submitting an application. Give them a heads-up with time to think. "I'm going to be applying for some jobs over the next few months and I'd love to ask if you'd be comfortable being a reference for me." Easy. Respectful. Almost everyone says yes.

Third, give them something to work with. If your kid has a résumé, share it. Let them know what the job or program is and why they want it. A good reference who knows what they're speaking to is far more useful than a great person who has no idea what to say.

Finally, say thank you — genuinely, specifically, after the fact. "Mr. Peters, I got the job. I really believe your reference made the difference. Thank you." That's the kind of follow-through that makes a reference want to do it again.

## Try this together

Sit down with your kid this week and do a quick **reference audit** — no judgment, just information.

Ask them to name every adult (outside your family) who has seen them do good work in the past two or three years. Think broadly: a boss, a coach, a teacher, a neighbor they helped, a youth program leader.

For each person they name, ask two questions together:
1. Would this person vouch for you right now, confidently and enthusiastically?
2. When did you last have any contact with them?

Then, for 2–3 of those people, identify one small thing your kid can do this month to strengthen the relationship. Not a big production — a thank-you email, a quick check-in, showing up more consistently if it's a current role.

If they can't name any adults outside the family who'd vouch for them, that's not a failure — it's information. It means the next job, team, or classroom is a chance to start building. Talk about one specific way they can do that.

## Questions to sit with

**For you (the parent):**
- Who are your own references right now — and when did you last check in with them?
- What did you model about leaving jobs or roles well? Would you want your kid to copy it?
- Is there an adult in your kid's life who clearly sees something in them — and does your kid know it?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- If someone called the adults in your life right now and asked about you, what would they say?
- Who is one person who has seen you work hard — and do they know you appreciate them?
- Is there a job, team, or class where you left without really saying goodbye? What would it look like to reconnect?
- What's one way you could be more "brag-worthy" to someone watching you right now?
