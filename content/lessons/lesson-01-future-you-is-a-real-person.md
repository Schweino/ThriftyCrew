# Week 1 — Future You Is a Real Person

*The choices your kid makes today aren't just habits — they're building materials for a life that's already under construction.*

**This week's big idea:** The adult your teenager will become is a real, specific person whose life is being shaped by the small, ordinary decisions happening right now. When you help your kid see that person clearly, everything changes.

## The Cartoon Problem

Here's the honest truth about being a teenager: the future doesn't feel real. When your kid is 14, "being 30" is basically a cartoon. It's foggy, distant, and irrelevant to what's happening on their phone right now.

That's not a character flaw. It's biology. The part of the brain that weighs long-term consequences is still being built — literally — into their mid-twenties. So when you say "think about your future," you're asking them to care about a person who, to them, barely exists.

The work this week isn't about money yet. It's about making Future Them feel real. That shift — from cartoon to person — is the foundation everything else is built on.

## Why Small Choices Are the Whole Game

Here's the part that surprises most people: life doesn't usually turn on one big dramatic moment. It turns on the quiet, ordinary decisions that don't look like decisions at all.

The kid who reads for 20 minutes every night. The teenager who shows up early to the job nobody's watching. The 16-year-old who saves $10 from every paycheck "for no reason." None of those feel important in the moment. But they're not adding up — they're compounding. More on that next week.

The flip side is equally true. The choices that feel harmless right now — blowing every dollar as it comes in, skipping the hard practice, letting "I'll do it later" become a personality trait — those are also building something. They're building a life too. Just not the one your kid would choose if they could see it clearly.

The question that cuts through all of this is simple: "Will Future Me thank me for this, or will Future Me be paying for it?"

That one question doesn't require a lecture. It doesn't require you to be the bad guy. You just ask it — casually, without drama — and let it do its work.

## This Isn't About Being Perfect

Before you read another word: this isn't about raising a kid who never makes a mistake. Nobody has done that, and nobody will.

It's also not about pressuring your kid to have their whole life planned out. That would backfire spectacularly, as you probably already know.

This is about something much smaller and more manageable: helping your teen develop the habit of considering their future self as a real person with real feelings, real preferences, and a real life that's being shaped right now.

Some weeks, they'll roll their eyes at this. That's fine. The seed still lands. I've seen it happen dozens of times — the kid who seemed completely checked out during the conversation brings it up six months later as if they thought of it themselves. Plant the seed. Don't stress about the harvest.

## Making Future You Feel Vivid

Abstract doesn't stick. Specific does.

"Think about your future" is abstract. "Close your eyes and picture where you want to live when you're 30 — do you have your own place?" is specific. The more concrete you make it, the more traction it gets.

Some families find it helps to name Future Them. Future Jake. Future Maya. It sounds a little silly, but it works — because it turns a foggy concept into someone with a name, and once there's a name, there's a relationship. And people take care of people they have a relationship with.

You can also borrow from someone already living it. Does your kid have an older cousin, neighbor, or family friend they respect — someone a decade or two ahead of them? Those people are priceless. The best lessons your kid will ever get about money and choices will come from someone they look up to who's already living the consequences of their early decisions, for better or worse. We'll come back to this idea a lot throughout the year.

## What This Year Is Going to Be

This membership gives you one lesson a week for 52 weeks. Each one is a practical tool — something you can actually bring to your teenager this week, not a theory to file away and forget.

But here's the honest version of how this works: your kid is not going to remember every lesson. They're not supposed to. What they will remember is the pattern of conversations, the habits you two built together, and the moments when things clicked. The lesson is just the doorway. The relationship is the room.

Some weeks will land perfectly. Some weeks, they'll grunt at you and disappear into their room, and you'll wonder why you're doing this. Keep going. The consistency is the whole point. Which, as you'll see next week, is exactly how compounding works.

For now, start with this: make Future Them real.

## Try this together

This week, find 15 quiet minutes with your teenager — not in the car necessarily, but somewhere you can both think without rushing.

Each of you picks an age. Something specific, like 28 or 30. Then each of you writes a short paragraph (5–8 sentences is plenty) describing a day in the life of Future You at that age.

Where do you live? What kind of work do you do — or want to do? What does your financial situation feel like? What are you proud of? What do you do on a Saturday morning?

Don't edit each other's. Don't push for the "right" answers. Just write, and then share.

Keep both paragraphs somewhere. A note on your phone, a folder on the counter, an envelope in a drawer. You'll come back to them at Week 52, and what your kid writes today will tell you something important about who they already are.

## Questions to sit with

**For you (the parent):**
- What's one decision you made between ages 14 and 22 that "future you" is still grateful for?
- What do you wish you'd started earlier — and what kept you from it?
- When you picture your kid at 30, what do you hope their relationship with money looks like?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- Who do you want to be at 30? Not your job title — just the kind of person. How do they feel day to day?
- Is there anything you're doing right now that you think Future You would be glad about?
- If Future You could send a text back to you today, what do you think it would say?
