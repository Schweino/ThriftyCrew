# Week 33 — How a Credit Card Actually Tricks You

*The trick isn't the interest rate — it's the minimum payment that makes everything feel fine.*

**This week's big idea:** A credit card doesn't trick your kid with complicated math. It tricks them with one tiny number at the bottom of the bill — the minimum payment — that makes a $500 purchase feel like a $15 problem.

## The Magic Trick, Explained

Here's how it works. Your kid buys something for $500 on a credit card. The statement arrives, and at the bottom it says something like: "Minimum payment due: $15."

Fifteen dollars. That feels fine. That feels manageable. That feels like they're handling it.

And that feeling — that's the trick.

The credit card company doesn't want your kid to pay the full $500. They want them to pay $15 a month. Because while that balance sits there, it earns them interest. And a typical credit card charges somewhere between 20% and 30% interest per year on whatever balance is left.

Here's what that actually costs. A $500 balance at 25% interest, paid off at only the minimum payment, takes roughly three years to clear — and your kid ends up paying somewhere around $640 or more for whatever that thing was. They paid 28% extra for a pair of shoes. Or a video game. Or a jacket.

Would they have paid $640 for a $500 item at the register if that's what the sticker said? Obviously not. The minimum payment hides the real price tag until the damage is done.

## The "30% Extra at the Register" Test

Here's a way to make this concrete for your teen. Next time they're about to buy something, ask them: "What if the cashier said it costs 30% more because of the payment method you chose? Would you still buy it?"

If the answer is no — and it usually is — then paying with a credit card and carrying a balance is exactly that. The difference is just that the cashier doesn't tell you. The bill arrives quietly a month later, split into comfortable little pieces.

This isn't about scaring your kid off credit cards. It's about helping them understand what the game is, so they can play it right.

## Two Ways to Use a Credit Card

There are really only two modes for a credit card, and they are completely different financial situations:

**Paid in full every month.** You use the card, the statement comes, you pay the entire balance before any interest is charged. In this case, you've essentially borrowed money for free for 30 days. Some cards even give you points or cash back. This is how credit cards can work in your favor — but only if you treat the card as a debit card that you pay off immediately, not a magic borrowing tool.

**Carrying a balance.** You don't pay the full amount when the bill comes. You pay the minimum, or something between the minimum and the full amount. Now interest starts accumulating on whatever's left. The longer it sits, the more it costs. And because minimums are designed to keep you in debt longer (that's how the card company makes money), the balance can hang around for years.

There is no middle ground here. Either the card is working for you, or it's working for them.

## The One Rule That Beats Every Trick

You don't need to teach your kid a complicated system. You just need to plant one rule deep enough that it becomes automatic:

**A credit card is not for buying what you don't already have the money for.**

That's it. If your kid has $200 in their account and they see something they want for $300, the credit card doesn't make that $300 theirs. It makes it a loan at 20-something percent interest. The money needs to exist first.

If they follow that one rule, every credit card trick becomes harmless. The minimum payment is irrelevant when you're paying the full balance. The interest rate is irrelevant when there's no balance to charge it to. The whole system that's designed to extract money from people loses its grip.

## But Credit Cards Aren't Evil

Here's what you don't want to do — turn this into a lecture that makes your kid afraid of credit cards entirely. That's its own kind of financial problem.

Used correctly — paid in full, every month, for spending you'd do anyway — a credit card can build credit history, offer fraud protection, and even earn small rewards. There are legitimate reasons adults use them.

The goal isn't for your teen to swear off credit forever. The goal is for them to understand how the system works before they sign up for anything. Because a lot of people spend years untangling debt they got into before they understood the game. Your kid doesn't have to do that. They can skip that part of the learning curve entirely — and that's worth more than any points or cash-back offer.

Future You, the adult your kid is building right now, will be so much freer without a credit card balance hanging over every month. The minimum payment trick only works on people who don't know it's a trick.

## Try this together

Pull up a free debt payoff calculator online — search "credit card minimum payment calculator" and several free options will come up.

Plug in a realistic scenario together: a $500 purchase, a 24% interest rate (a common rate for student or starter cards), and the minimum payment option. Let the calculator show you the total cost and the payoff timeline.

Then run it again with "pay the balance in full" and compare.

You don't need to make a big point. Just let the numbers do the talking. Ask your teen: "What would you rather do with the extra money you'd be paying in interest?" Let them answer. That's the lesson landing.

If you want to go further, find a real credit card offer you've received (junk mail is great for this) and read the fine print together — specifically the APR section. Make the terms visible and normal to discuss, not scary.

## Questions to sit with

**For you (the parent):**
- Have you ever carried a balance on a credit card, and if so, how long did it take to pay off? What did it actually cost you?
- What's the mental model you use for your own credit card — do you treat it like a debit card, or something else?
- Is there anything about your own credit card habits you'd want your kid to do differently?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- If a store charged you 25% extra just for using a certain payment method, would you still use it?
- What does "minimum payment" actually mean — and why do you think the company makes the minimum so low?
- What's the one rule that makes a credit card work for you instead of against you?
