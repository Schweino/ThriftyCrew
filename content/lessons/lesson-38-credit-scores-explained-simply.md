# Week 38 — Credit Scores, Explained Simply

*A credit score is just your reputation for money — and your kid is already laying the groundwork for it, whether they know it or not.*

**This week's big idea:** A credit score isn't a mysterious grade handed down from somewhere — it's a track record built by consistent, honest behavior with borrowed money over time. Understand how it's built, what hurts it, and how a young adult starts from zero without panic.

## What a Credit Score Actually Is

Let's strip away the mystery.

A credit score is a three-digit number — typically between 300 and 850 — that lenders, landlords, and sometimes even employers use to quickly judge how reliably you pay back what you owe. The higher the number, the better. A score above 700 is generally considered good. Above 750, most doors open comfortably. Below 600, borrowing money gets expensive or difficult.

Back in Week 20, we talked about reputation — how it's built by consistent daily behavior stored in other people's memories, and how it opens or closes doors before you ever walk into the room. A credit score is exactly that, but in numerical form. It is a reputation for money.

The same rules apply. You build it slowly, through small honest actions repeated over time. You can damage it quickly with a few bad moves. And once it's strong, it quietly works for you in places you never expected.

## Why It Actually Matters (Real Consequences)

Here is where parents sometimes lose the room when talking to teens: the credit score feels abstract because the consequences feel far away. So let's make them concrete.

**Apartments.** Most landlords check credit before renting to you. A low score — or no score — means paying a larger security deposit, needing a co-signer, or losing an apartment to someone with a better score.

**Car loans.** The interest rate you pay on a car loan is directly tied to your credit score. Someone with excellent credit might borrow $15,000 at 5% interest. Someone with poor credit might pay 18% or more for the same loan. On a $15,000 loan over five years, that difference is thousands of dollars paid to the lender for no reason other than an undeveloped credit history.

**Mortgages.** A small difference in mortgage interest rate, multiplied over 30 years on a significant loan, can easily add up to $50,000 or more in extra interest paid over the life of the loan.

None of these things feel immediate to a sixteen-year-old. But they will, and sooner than feels possible right now. Planting the seed today is exactly the kind of thing Future You will be grateful for.

## What Builds It and What Hurts It

Credit scores are calculated using several factors, but two of them carry the most weight by far.

**Payment history** is the biggest piece — roughly 35% of most scoring models. It is simple: do you pay what you owe, on time, every time? One missed payment can knock a score down meaningfully. A long track record of on-time payments is the single most powerful thing a person can do for their score. This is just reliability showing up in financial form.

**Credit utilization** is the second big one — roughly 30%. This is how much of your available credit you're actually using at any given time. If you have a credit card with a $1,000 limit and you carry a $900 balance, that's 90% utilization, which scores badly. Staying below 30% of your limit — and ideally much lower — signals that you're not dependent on borrowed money to get through the month.

Other factors include the length of your credit history (older accounts help), the mix of types of credit (a mortgage plus a car loan plus a card looks more robust than one card), and how many new accounts you've opened recently (opening several credit cards in a short window looks like financial stress).

What hurts it: missed or late payments, maxing out cards, closing old accounts (it shortens your history), and applying for lots of new credit at once.

## Starting from Zero: How a Young Adult Begins Building Credit

Your teen currently has no credit score. That's completely normal. But starting to build it at 18 — or earlier with the right tools — means by their mid-twenties, they'll have a track record that opens doors instead of closing them.

Here are three legitimate ways to start.

**A secured credit card** is the most common starting point. A secured card requires a cash deposit (often $200–$500) that becomes the card's credit limit. Use it for small purchases — gas, groceries — and pay the full balance every single month before the due date. That's it. After 12–18 months of on-time payments, most issuers will upgrade to a regular card and return the deposit.

**Becoming an authorized user** on a parent's account is another option. If you have a credit card with a solid payment history, adding your teen as an authorized user (you don't have to give them the physical card) can let some of that history show up on their credit report. This one comes with responsibility — their actions can affect your score too, so it's a trust-based move.

**A credit-builder loan** is offered by many credit unions and community banks specifically to help people establish credit. You make small monthly payments into an account; at the end, you receive the money. It's less about the cash and more about the track record it builds.

The golden rule is simple, no matter which path: charge only what you can pay in full that month. A credit card is not for buying things you don't have the money for. Every lesson we've covered about debt applies here too.

## Your Credit Report: The Document Behind the Number

A credit score is a snapshot calculated from your credit report — a detailed record of every account you've opened, every payment you've made or missed, and any public financial records.

Everyone is entitled to a free copy of their credit report from each of the three major bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) once per year through AnnualCreditReport.com. Many banks and credit card apps now show your score for free as well.

It's worth checking your own report, for two reasons. First, errors happen — and an error on your report can drag down your score unfairly. Second, checking it is a habit that keeps you honest and informed. Knowing where you stand is always better than guessing.

## Try this together

This week, do two things.

First, share something real with your kid about how credit has affected your own life. Not a lecture — a story. Maybe it's the rate you got on your car or house. Maybe it's the first apartment you applied for and how the credit check felt. Make it concrete and human, not abstract and teacherly.

Second, look up your credit score together using your bank app, credit card app, or a free service. Show your kid what a real score looks like — and talk through one or two things on it (an account age, a payment history) that explain why the number is where it is. If they're approaching 18, talk about which of the three building strategies makes the most sense to start with.

You're not trying to teach a financial literacy textbook tonight. You're just making this feel real and manageable, instead of mysterious and scary.

## Questions to sit with

**For you (the parent):**
- Do you know what's actually on your credit report right now — and when did you last check it?
- Is there something in your own credit history you wish you'd handled differently at a younger age that would be worth sharing honestly with your kid?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- How is a credit score similar to the reputation we talked about a few months ago — and how is it different?
- If you could do one thing in the next two years to make sure you have a solid credit score at 20, what would it be?
- Why do you think paying on time every single time matters so much more than any other single factor?
