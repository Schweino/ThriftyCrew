**This week's big idea:** A first interview isn't a test of how impressive your kid is — it's a test of whether they're prepared and easy to work with. A few practiced answers and some basic manners will beat most other applicants.

## Why Interviews Feel Scarier Than They Are

The first interview feels enormous because it's unknown. Your kid is walking into a room where a stranger is going to ask questions they don't know how to answer, while they try to look calm, remember what they practiced, avoid saying "um" too much, and figure out where to put their hands. No wonder it's terrifying.

But here's what you can tell them: **the bar for a first job interview is genuinely not that high.** Most hiring managers for entry-level teen positions are looking for very basic signals — can this person hold a conversation? Do they seem like they'll show up? Will they get along with the rest of the team? Are they going to be a problem?

That's it. The candidate who walks in prepared, makes decent eye contact, answers questions with a little thought, and thanks the interviewer at the end has already done more than half the competition.

The goal isn't perfect. The goal is prepared.

## What to Wear and How to Arrive

This sounds basic, but it's where a lot of teens stumble. For a first job interview — even somewhere casual like a restaurant or retail store — your kid should dress one level above what they'd wear on the job.

If the job is casual, dress business casual: clean clothes, no graphic tees, nothing torn or wrinkled. Hair neat. Shoes that look like they tried. If the job is more customer-facing, dress slightly more formal — not a suit, but something polished.

The rule is simple: look like you took this seriously.

On timing: arrive five to ten minutes early, not thirty. Sit quietly, put the phone away, and be pleasant to whoever you interact with — the host, the cashier, the receptionist. Hiring managers ask those people what they noticed. You'd be surprised how often it matters.

## The Questions They'll Ask (and How to Answer Them)

For a first job, most interviews are short — fifteen to twenty minutes — and the questions are pretty predictable. Here are the ones your teen is most likely to face, with a frame for how to answer each:

**"Tell me about yourself."** This isn't an invitation for a life story. It's an opening for a thirty-second introduction: your name, your school year, one or two things you like or do, and why you're interested in this job. Keep it friendly and brief.

**"Why do you want to work here?"** Be honest but specific. "I've eaten here a bunch and I like the environment" is fine. "I want to start earning my own money and this seemed like a good place to learn" is also fine. What doesn't land: "I just need money." Even if true, say it with a better frame.

**"Do you have any experience?"** For a first job, the honest answer is probably no formal experience — and that's okay to say. But pivot quickly: "I don't have a formal job yet, but I've been [babysitting / helping at school events / mowing lawns] and I'm a quick learner. I'm serious about being reliable."

**"What are your strengths?"** Coach your kid to pick two or three genuine ones and give a tiny example. "I'm pretty good at staying calm when things get busy" or "I'm on time — I don't like being late." Real, specific, brief.

**"What are your weaknesses?"** The honest-but-not-harmful approach: name something real that you're actively improving. "I can be too quiet when I'm nervous, but I'm working on speaking up more." Avoid "I work too hard." Nobody buys it.

**"When are you available?"** Know this in advance. Have a clear, honest answer: "I'm available after 3 p.m. on school days and all day on weekends."

## What To Do At the End

Before the interview closes, your kid should do two things:

First, **ask one thoughtful question.** This is one of the clearest signals of a prepared, interested candidate. Good options: "What do the best employees here have in common?" or "What would a typical first week look like?" or "Is this a role where I could take on more responsibility over time?" One question is enough. Two is fine. Three starts to feel like an interrogation.

Second, **thank them directly.** Look the person in the eye, shake their hand if it feels right, and say: "Thank you for your time — I'm really interested in this position." Simple. Warm. Done.

If they said to expect to hear back within a week, follow up on exactly day seven if you haven't heard. A brief, polite email or call is not pushy — it shows continued interest.

## Nerves Are Normal (And Not the Enemy)

Your teen needs to hear this: nerves are not a sign that they're not ready. They're a sign that they care. Almost every adult who interviews well still gets nervous — they've just learned to breathe through it and keep going anyway.

One practical trick: the night before, have your kid do three or four reps of answering the questions out loud. Not rehearsing a script word-for-word, but getting comfortable saying things out loud so it doesn't feel foreign in the room. The brain does better when something feels familiar.

Another trick: remind them that the interviewer wants to hire someone. They're not sitting there looking for reasons to say no. They have a shift to fill and they're hoping the next person who walks in is the right one. Walk in like you might be that person.

## Try this together

This week, run a ten-minute mock interview at the kitchen table. You play the hiring manager. Your kid sits across from you — not on the couch, actually at a table — and answers the questions as if it's real.

Use at least five of these questions: Tell me about yourself. Why do you want to work here? What are your strengths? What's a weakness you're working on? When are you available? What would you do if a customer was rude to you?

After the mock interview, give feedback gently. Not "that was bad" but "you looked down a lot — try to meet my eyes more" or "that answer was great, you could trim it by half and it would land even better." Then let them try one or two answers again.

At the end, switch roles if your teen is willing — let them interview you. It helps them see the other side of the table and takes some of the mystery out of the process.

## Questions to sit with

**For yourself:**
- When you face something high-stakes — an interview, a pitch, a hard conversation — do you actually prepare and practice out loud, or do you wing it and hope the nerves settle on their own?
- If someone asked you right now "why should I pick you?" — for a job, a client, an opportunity — what's your honest, specific answer, and do you believe it?
- What's one nerve-inducing thing on your horizon that would feel far smaller if you simply ran a few practice reps of it this week?

**For you (the parent):**
- What interview advice do you wish someone had given you before your first one? Have you shared that with your kid?
- How do you handle your own nerves before something high-stakes, and can you share that strategy with your teen?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- Which interview question feels scariest to you, and what would your honest, best answer actually be?
- If the interviewer asked "why should I hire you?" — what would you say, and do you believe it?
- What would "walking out of that interview feeling good about yourself" look like to you?
