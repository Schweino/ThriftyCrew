# Week 48 — The Few Expensive Mistakes

*Most mistakes are tuition. They cost a little, teach a lot, and your kid should make plenty of them. But a handful of mistakes are a different category — they bill you for years. This week is about helping your kid see that aisle coming.*

**This week's big idea:** Your job isn't to protect your kid from every mistake — that would backfire badly. Your job is to let them fail cheaply and often on the small stuff, while helping them recognize the few mistakes that are genuinely hard to undo. Those are the ones worth slowing down for.

## The Mistake You Want Your Kid to Make

Before we talk about the expensive ones, let's say clearly: failing is good.

Bombing a test and having to study harder next time is good. Buying a cheap gadget that breaks in a week is good. Taking a job and realizing they hate the industry is good. Overspending their allowance and having nothing left for the thing they actually wanted — good. Trying something creative that doesn't work out, falling off the bike, making the wrong call on a group project — all of it, good.

Every one of those mistakes is cheap. It has a cost that fits inside a week, a grade, a paycheck. The lesson lands fast, the pain is proportional, and life moves on. Your kid learns something that no lecture could have taught as well. That's exactly how it's supposed to work.

The mistake most parents make is trying to protect their kid from this entire category. That's the overcorrection. Kids who've been shielded from every small failure arrive at adulthood without the emotional calluses they need to handle the bigger ones. Let the cheap mistakes happen. Resist the urge to catch every fall.

The category worth your attention is different.

## What Makes a Mistake "Expensive"

An expensive mistake has a few defining features, and it's worth being clear about them.

**It's hard to undo.** Some choices, once made, open a door and close another one behind you. Not every door — your kid can always rebuild, redirect, start over. But some choices take real time to reverse, and that time costs something.

**The consequences keep running.** A cheap mistake ends when it ends. An expensive mistake creates a chain. A financial decision that commits future income for years. A reputation event that follows a kid from one chapter into the next. A choice made under pressure in a single moment that reshapes what the next several years look like.

**The gap between the decision and the damage is huge.** This is what makes expensive mistakes invisible in the moment. They don't look big when you make them. They look ordinary — even reasonable. The size only reveals itself later, when the bill arrives and it's larger than anyone expected.

Your kid doesn't need a list of every possible expensive mistake. They need a way of *recognizing the pattern* — so they can pause when they're standing in front of one, even if nobody's told them specifically about that one.

## The Moment of Pressure

Here's the pattern that shows up most consistently in expensive mistakes: they happen when a decision needs to be made fast, in front of other people, under the feeling that hesitation will cost you something social.

That moment — the moment when everyone else seems to already know what they're doing, when slowing down feels like weakness, when going along is easy and pushing back is awkward — is the exact moment the most expensive decisions get made.

Your kid will face this. They already face it, probably more often than you know. The pressure to go along with an impulsive money move because everyone else is doing it. The pressure to spend what they haven't got because the group is doing the thing tonight. The pressure to make a big decision now because the window seems like it's closing.

The single most useful thing you can give your kid for these moments isn't a rule. Rules aren't fast enough. What's fast enough is a *question*: **"Is this hard to undo?"**

That's it. One question. If the answer is yes — if the thing they're about to do will be genuinely difficult to reverse — then it deserves more than a split second. It deserves a pause, a conversation with someone they trust, at minimum a night of sleep. Not because hesitation is always right, but because the cost of slowing down on a reversible decision is almost nothing, while the cost of rushing into an irreversible one can be enormous.

## Big Impulsive Money Moves

Let's be specific about one category that bites teenagers hard: major financial decisions made in the moment, on the feeling, without time or numbers.

This shows up in all kinds of forms at your kid's age. The car that seems like a deal until the payment plan is actually looked at. The big-ticket purchase made on impulse because of how it looked, not because of whether it was affordable. The financial commitment made in the company of friends who made it feel exciting rather than weighty. The thing bought to keep up — to not be the one who doesn't have it — that immediately starts billing your kid for months afterward.

The pattern is the same in all of them: the emotion is in the room, the numbers aren't. The decision is made fast, socially, under the feeling that it's exciting or urgent. The consequences arrive slow.

Going back to the definition of "affordable" from a few weeks ago: if your kid can't pay for it in full right now, they can't afford it — yet. That's not a life sentence. It just means wait. Save. Come back. But making a large, committed, hard-to-exit financial move because the moment felt electric is how expensive mistakes in the money category get made.

## Choices That Follow You

The other category of expensive mistake worth naming — gently and without drama — is the choice that creates a record.

Your kid is at an age where their digital footprint, their public behavior, and the impressions they make on the adults in their life are all forming a reputation that will travel with them. Most of it is fine. Most of it is forgotten by next week.

But some things don't get forgotten. The moment that ends up in a story that follows them. The comment in a public space. The behavior in a setting where the wrong person sees it. The decision made in front of the wrong audience.

This isn't about fear. And it absolutely isn't about living under surveillance or trying to be perfect. It's about the same question: is this hard to undo? Would this choice feel the same if a teacher, a future employer, or someone they respect were watching?

Reputation travels — we've talked about that throughout the year. And the flip side of reputation being something you can build on purpose is that it can also be damaged in a single moment that takes a long time to repair. That's the version of reputation worth talking about this week.

## Try this together

This week, do a concrete exercise together — keep it light, no lecture energy needed. Call it the **Cheap vs. Expensive game**.

Take turns naming mistakes. Real ones, hypothetical ones, both. For each one, ask: *Is this cheap or expensive?*

Some examples to get you started:
- Spending your whole paycheck and having nothing left for gas — *cheap* (you learn, you recover, next paycheck is coming)
- Putting a big purchase on a credit card you can't fully pay off — *depends, but could be expensive if it becomes a habit*
- Making a major financial commitment based on how something felt in the moment, without running the numbers — *expensive*
- Trying a new job and realizing you don't like it — *cheap* (you know something you didn't know before)
- A public moment of bad judgment that people are still bringing up months later — *expensive*
- Bombing one test — *cheap*
- Dropping a class you needed and having to retake it, delaying plans — *a little expensive, but recoverable*

The goal isn't to frighten your kid about every decision. It's to build the mental category. Once your kid knows what "expensive" feels like as a pattern — rushed, social, irreversible, numbers-free — they can spot it in the wild, in the moment it matters.

Agree at the end: cheap mistakes are allowed. They're required, even. Expensive ones deserve a pause, a question, and at minimum one more conversation before going in.

## Questions to sit with

**For you (the parent):**
- What's a cheap mistake you made as a teenager that taught you something you still carry? Have you shared that one with your kid?
- What's an expensive mistake you made — financial or otherwise — that you wish you'd slowed down on? Not for confession's sake, but because your kid hearing it once might save them from learning it the hard way.
- Are there areas of your kid's life right now where you see the setup for an expensive mistake forming? Is there a low-pressure way to name it without making it an ultimatum?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- What's a mistake you've made that you'd totally make again — because you learned something real from it?
- What's an example of a choice that seems like a small decision in the moment but could actually be hard to undo?
- When you're in a situation where everyone else is moving fast and you feel pressure to decide quickly — what would it look like to actually pause?
