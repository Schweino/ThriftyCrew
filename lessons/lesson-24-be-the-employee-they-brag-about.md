# Week 24 — Be the Employee They Brag About

*The real payoff from a first job isn't the paycheck. It's the person your boss becomes willing to call on your behalf.*

**This week's big idea:** Good references come from being the worker a boss can't wait to recommend — and building that reputation starts from the very first shift, not after years on the job.

## What Happens When You're Not in the Room

Here's something worth sitting with: your kid's boss is thinking about them when they're not there. Not in a dramatic way — just the normal, quiet way managers think about their team. Who can I count on for extra hours? Who would I trust with the new hire? Who do I want on the register when it gets busy?

These mental notes are being written from day one. Every shift, every interaction, every moment when it would have been easier to do the minimum — your teen is either earning a spot in the "brag about" column or drifting toward "reliable enough, I guess."

The good news: getting into the brag column isn't complicated. It doesn't require being the most talented, the fastest, or the most experienced. It requires a handful of habits that are entirely within your kid's control. And because most people don't bother, the bar is lower than it sounds.

## The Difference Between the Minimum and Brag-Worthy

Every job has a minimum. Clock in. Do the tasks. Clock out. The minimum is what keeps you employed. It's not what gets you recommended.

Brag-worthy is different, and it usually shows up in small ways:

**Initiative.** The minimum is doing your assigned tasks. Brag-worthy is noticing something that needs to be done and doing it without being asked. The trash is full — don't wait for someone to tell you. The display looks messy — straighten it between customers. The new hire looks lost — help them. These aren't heroic acts. They're just eyes-open behavior, and managers notice every time.

**Attitude.** The minimum is not having a bad attitude. Brag-worthy is being genuinely pleasant to work with — especially on a hard shift, especially with a difficult customer, especially when the manager isn't watching. The attitude your kid brings when things are easy doesn't tell the boss much. The attitude they bring when things are annoying tells the boss everything.

**Reliability.** We talked about this in Week 21. But it applies here too. Showing up on time, every time. Not calling out unless there's a real reason. Covering a shift when asked. Following through on what they said they'd do. This is the foundation that everything else sits on. An employee with initiative and a great attitude who's chronically late is a headache. An employee who's dependable in every small way is a treasure — and bosses know it.

**Asking good questions.** There's a version of asking questions that signals weakness, and there's a version that signals engagement. "I wasn't sure how you want this done — can you show me once so I get it right?" is the engaged version. It tells the boss: this person wants to do it right, not just get through the shift.

## Why the First Job's Real Payoff Is the Reference

The paycheck from a first job matters — earning your own money is a real milestone, and your teen should feel proud of it. But over the long arc of your kid's life, that hourly wage is going to matter very little.

What's going to matter is the reference.

When your teen eventually applies for a second job, or a college program, or an internship, or anything with more responsibility — the question every decision-maker is going to ask is: "Who vouches for this person?" A first boss who says *"I'd hire them back without hesitating"* is worth more than a polished résumé. A first boss who says *"they were fine, showed up most of the time"* barely moves the needle.

The reference is earned over the whole tenure of the job. It's built shift by shift, choice by choice. And here's the part that makes it feel worth it: it doesn't require being extraordinary. It just requires being consistent. Consistent reliability, consistent effort, consistent attitude. That's rare. That's what gets you bragged about.

## How Bosses Talk About Employees They Love

If you've ever managed anyone, you know exactly what this sounds like. You get a call from someone asking about a former employee, and either you light up — "Oh, yes, let me tell you about her, she was fantastic" — or you choose your words carefully because you're trying to be honest without being unkind.

Your kid needs to understand that the conversation their boss has after they leave a job is already being shaped by what they do today. Not in some distant hypothetical way — actively, right now.

The things that make a boss light up: "Always on time." "Never had to ask twice." "Went out of their way." "Never complained about the hard shifts." "The customers loved her." "I knew if I gave him a task, it would be done."

None of those phrases are about talent. None of them are about being the smartest or the fastest. They're all about character, showing up, and caring about the work. Your teen already has the capacity for every single one of those things.

## What If They Don't Have a Job Yet?

The principles in this lesson apply everywhere your teen is performing and being observed. A teacher who can't wait to recommend a student. A coach who brags about an athlete's work ethic to the next coach. A neighbor who tells their friends about the most reliable kid they've ever hired to mow their lawn.

If your kid isn't employed yet, take this lesson and apply it to the environment they're already in. School, team, volunteer roles, family responsibilities. Ask: are they brag-worthy there? Is there a teacher who'd say "this kid is the kind of student I want in my classroom?" If yes, wonderful. If not, that's something to work on — and working on it now costs nothing.

The reputation your teen is building isn't limited to a job. It travels with them everywhere they go.

## Try this together

If your teen has a job right now, sit down together this week and name two concrete, specific things they could do on their next shift that go beyond the minimum. Not vague things like "try harder" — specific things, like "I'll stock the shelves without being asked" or "I'll stay at the end of my shift to help close down the prep station." Write them down. Check in after the shift.

If your teen doesn't have a job yet, apply the same exercise to school or an activity. Name two ways they could go beyond what's required this week, in a place where a trusted adult would notice. Then do those two things and report back.

Either way, the goal is the same: practice becoming brag-worthy in the small moments, because that's when the habit is built.

## Questions to sit with

**For you (the parent):**
- Would a past boss — or a current employer, a teacher, a coach — brag about you? What would they say? Is that the example you want to be setting?
- Is there something you're modeling at home around effort and attitude that you'd want your kid to carry into their work life?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- What would your current boss, teacher, or coach say about you right now — honestly? Would they brag, or would they choose their words carefully?
- What's one specific thing you could do this week, at your job or at school, that would make someone say "that kid goes above and beyond"?
- If your boss had to describe you to someone hiring you next, what would you want them to say — and are you making it easy for them to say it?
