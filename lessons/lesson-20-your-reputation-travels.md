# Week 20 — Your Reputation Travels Faster Than You Do

*Long before your kid walks into a room, their reputation is already in the chair waiting.*

**This week's big idea:** Reputation isn't something that happens to your kid — it's something they're building right now, one action at a time, stored in the memory of every person they've ever interacted with. It compounds. It opens doors they'll never see coming — and closes doors before they know they existed.

## What a Reputation Actually Is

Here's the clearest way to think about it: **reputation is the sum of your daily behavior stored in other people's memories.**

Not what you intend. Not what you believe about yourself. What other people have actually experienced from you, repeatedly, over time.

When a manager needs to recommend someone for a better shift, they don't think about who tried hardest in their head. They think about who showed up consistently, handled things without being asked, and didn't leave them in a lurch. That stored memory — not the intention — is the reputation.

When a teacher writes a college recommendation, they write about what they've seen. When a coach gives a player more responsibility, it's because of what that player's track record says. When a neighbor refers your teen for a job, they're putting their own reputation on the line based on what they believe your kid's behavior will predict.

All of that is reputation. And the thing about it that most teenagers don't fully understand: it's already forming. Right now. At school, at work, at practice, even at home. Every action your kid takes in front of another person is a deposit or a withdrawal in an account they may not know they have.

## "Do What You Say, 100% of the Time"

There is one behavior that builds a strong reputation faster than almost anything else, and it sounds almost insultingly simple: **do what you say you'll do.**

Tell your boss you'll be there at 4:00 — be there at 3:55. Tell a friend you'll handle something — handle it. Commit to finishing a task — finish it. Say "yes, I'll do that" — and then actually do that.

This sounds like the floor, not the ceiling. But here's the truth: the people who consistently do what they say, 100% of the time — not 80%, not "usually" — are genuinely rare. So rare that when someone finds a person who operates that way, they hold onto them. They give them responsibility. They recommend them. They trust them with the better opportunities.

Your teenager lives in a world where the standard is often "close enough" — turn in the homework mostly on time, show up mostly when you said you would, follow through most of the time. The gap between "close enough" and "100% of the time" is not as wide as it sounds. And the advantage that gap creates is enormous.

This isn't about being perfect. Everyone misses sometimes. But the person who takes their commitments seriously enough to communicate when something goes wrong, to follow up, to be the one others can count on — that person is building something that most people never do.

## It Compounds — In Both Directions

Here's what makes reputation feel different from other kinds of success: it compounds.

A strong reputation opens a door. Walking through that door — and performing well — adds to the reputation. The expanded reputation opens a bigger door. Repeat.

In the other direction: a reputation for unreliability closes a door. Having fewer opportunities makes the next impression harder. The pattern of "they weren't dependable" sticks around much longer than most teens expect.

The compounding works slowly enough that it's invisible in the moment. Your teen won't notice their reputation building week over week. But in six months, or two years, or when they apply for a better job or a scholarship or a reference, it shows up all at once — and it either works for them or against them in ways they can trace directly back to the behavior they put in the bank.

This is the same logic as money compounding. The actions today are the deposits. The results down the road are the interest. Start early, and the math works powerfully in your favor.

## It Travels Before You Do

Here's the part that surprises teenagers most.

Managers talk to other managers. Teachers talk to other teachers. Coaches compare notes. The owner of the restaurant where your kid works may know the owner of another restaurant. The adult who observed your teen at a volunteer event may sit on the hiring committee somewhere else.

Long before your kid shows up to apply for something, their reputation may already be in the building. This can work wonderfully — "Oh, I know that kid, they're great" — or it can be the door that closes before they ever knocked.

This isn't meant to be scary. It's meant to be empowering. Because the flip side of "your reputation travels" is "you get to decide what travels." You're sending something ahead of you everywhere you go. Make it something worth sending.

## What They're Known for Right Now

This is the practical question, and it's worth asking your teen directly: **What are you currently known for?**

Not in a challenging way — in a genuinely curious way. At school, what do people associate with your name? On the team, what's your reputation among coaches? At your job, how does your manager describe you to someone else?

Some of this is conscious and chosen. A lot of it isn't. Most people — adults included — have never thought deliberately about their reputation. They've just been behaving, and the reputation forms in the background.

The invitation this week is to make it conscious. To help your teen ask: what is my name meaning to people right now? And is that what I want it to mean?

If the answer is yes, that's worth celebrating. If the answer is "I'm not sure" or "not really," that's useful information — because reputation is buildable. It changes. Every consistent action from now forward is a vote for the reputation your teen wants to carry.

## Try this together

Have a conversation this week — low-key, not a lecture — that goes something like this:

"I want to ask you something, and I'm not going to make a big deal of it. What do you think you're currently known for — at school, at work, or anywhere you spend time regularly? Not what you want to be known for. What you think you actually are known for right now."

Let them answer. Really listen. Don't correct or redirect immediately.

Then ask the second question: "What would you want your name to mean to people?" Let them describe it.

Then bridge: "What's one thing you could do differently this week that would move toward that?"

Write that one thing down together. One specific, concrete action. Not a theme — an action. "Be on time every day this week." "Follow through on the thing I told my manager I'd do." "Respond when people reach out instead of leaving them hanging." One thing, for one week. That's the start.

## Questions to sit with

**For you (the parent):**
- What are you known for, among the people in your professional and personal life — and did that reputation happen on purpose or by default?
- Think back to when you were your kid's age: did you understand then that your reputation was already forming? What would you have done differently if you had?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- If your best friend described you to someone who's never met you, what would they say? What about your boss or a teacher — would their description be different?
- What do you want your name to mean to people when you're not in the room?
- Think of one person whose reputation you admire — someone people speak highly of without being asked. What specific behaviors do you think built that reputation?
