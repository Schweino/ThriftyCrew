# Week 37 — Student Loans Without the Panic

*This week we take the biggest debt your kid will likely ever face and make it something you can actually plan for together.*

**This week's big idea:** Student loans are not inevitable doom — but they are a serious commitment that deserves serious thought before signing. The families who come out ahead are the ones who look at the real numbers early, borrow only what's truly needed, and don't wait until graduation to start chipping away.

## The Number Nobody Wants to Say Out Loud

Let's start by naming what we're actually talking about. The average student borrower in the United States graduates with somewhere north of $30,000 in debt. That's not pocket change. For some fields and some schools, it's two or three times that.

And here's the part that catches people off guard: the interest doesn't wait politely until you graduate to start running. On most unsubsidized loans, interest accumulates the whole time your kid is in school. They graduate, and the balance is already larger than the original loan amount. That's not a punishment — it's just math. The kind of math that rewards people who understand it and punishes people who don't look at it until it's too late.

That's why this conversation happens now, before any loan paperwork gets signed. Not because loans are shameful. Not because college isn't worth it. But because informed families make smarter decisions.

## College Is Not the Only Path — and That's Worth Saying Clearly

Before we get into how loans work, let's spend one honest minute on this: college is one excellent road. It is not the only road.

Skilled trades — electricians, plumbers, HVAC technicians, welders, dental hygienists — are desperately needed, often pay extremely well, and frequently require two years or less of training at a fraction of the cost of a four-year degree. If your kid is drawn to hands-on work, entrepreneurship, or a field that doesn't require a specific credential, it's worth genuinely exploring that path rather than defaulting to the assumption that four years of college is non-negotiable.

This isn't about discouraging ambition. It's about matching the investment to the return and to the kid in front of you. A student who attends college with a clear goal and a plan to minimize debt is in a very different position than one who is there because it seemed like the next step.

## How Student Loans Actually Work

Here is the simple version.

Federal student loans come from the government. They typically have lower interest rates and more repayment flexibility than private loans. They come in two main types: subsidized (the government covers the interest while you're in school if you qualify based on financial need) and unsubsidized (interest starts building immediately). Private loans come from banks and other lenders — they can have higher rates and fewer protections.

The FAFSA — Free Application for Federal Student Aid — is the form that determines what federal aid your kid qualifies for. Fill it out early, every year they're in school. It unlocks grants (free money, no repayment), work-study opportunities, and subsidized loans. This is not optional paperwork. It's the front door to a significant amount of help.

When loan offers arrive, read them. The interest rate, the loan type, and the total borrowed amount are the numbers that matter. Accepting the maximum offered loan amount "just in case" is how families end up with debt they didn't need.

## The Rule That Changes Everything: Don't Wait Until Graduation

Here is the single most actionable thing in this entire lesson.

If your kid works at all during school — a part-time job, a summer gig, anything — and they put even a modest amount toward their loan interest while they're still in school, they graduate in a fundamentally different position than their peers.

Not making full payments. Not heroic sacrifice. Just paying the interest as it accrues — or more when possible — so the balance doesn't silently balloon. Even $50 or $100 a month during the school years keeps the original borrowed amount from ballooning. It's the compounding principle in reverse: stop the interest from stacking, and the payoff timeline shrinks dramatically.

This is the kind of move that Future You will thank you for. The twenty-two-year-old who graduates with roughly what they borrowed, rather than $5,000–$10,000 more, has a head start that matters.

## The Return-on-Investment Conversation

Here is a useful frame for any specific school and major combination your kid is considering: what is the realistic starting salary in that field, and how does it compare to the total cost of attending that school for four years?

This is not a cold, purely financial calculation. There are real reasons to attend a school that costs more, or to pursue a field that doesn't pay the most. But those decisions should be made with eyes open, not made by default because a school looked great on a campus visit.

A rough rule of thumb that many financial educators suggest: total student loan debt at graduation should ideally stay below the first year's expected salary in that field. So if a field typically pays $40,000 to start, borrowing $80,000 for school is a very different conversation than borrowing $35,000. That's not a hard line — it's a thinking tool. Use it.

Scholarships, grants, community college for the first two years, in-state tuition, and living at home can all dramatically reduce the total borrowed. These options aren't consolation prizes. They're smart moves that Future You will appreciate enormously.

## Try this together

If college is somewhere on your kid's radar — even a few years away — do this exercise together this week. Pick one school your kid has mentioned or is curious about. Look up the total annual cost (tuition, fees, room, board — the real number). Then look up the median starting salary for the field your kid is considering. You can find both with a basic search.

Write down two numbers side by side: total cost for four years versus likely first-year salary. Now ask: if we borrowed everything, how long would it take to pay it off? And what would we need to do — scholarships, working during school, starting at a community college — to close that gap?

You don't need to have answers tonight. The goal is to make the conversation real and number-based, not abstract. "College is expensive" is something a teen can ignore. A specific dollar figure sitting next to a specific salary is something they can think about.

## Questions to sit with

**For you (the parent):**
- What do you wish you had known about student loans before you (or your kid) started making decisions about school?
- Have you had a concrete conversation with your kid about the actual cost of the schools they're interested in — or has it mostly been hopes and rankings?
- Are there options — trades, community college, work-study, scholarships — you haven't fully explored together yet?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- If you had to describe in one sentence what you want your life to look like at 25, what would you say? What path gets you there?
- What's one thing you could do during school years to make sure you graduate with as little debt as possible?
- By the real definition of "affordable," can you afford the school you're most excited about right now — and if not, what's the plan to make it work?
