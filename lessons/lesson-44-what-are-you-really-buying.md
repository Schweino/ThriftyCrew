# Week 44 — What Are You Really Buying?

*Most spending isn't about the thing. Help your kid name what they're actually after.*

**This week's big idea:** A lot of purchases are really attempts to buy a feeling — belonging, status, confidence, comfort, or an escape. When a kid (or parent) can name what they're actually trying to get, they get a much better shot at deciding whether the purchase will actually deliver it. And most of the time, it won't.

## The Price Tag Isn't the Whole Story

Here's something that almost never gets talked about in money conversations, even though it explains most spending decisions: we are almost never just buying the thing.

When your kid needs the exact same brand of shoes that certain people at school wear, they are not buying shoes. They have shoes. They are buying belonging — or the attempt at it. When someone upgrades their phone the moment a new model drops even though the old one works perfectly, they are not buying a phone. They are buying the feeling of being current, of being the person who has the latest thing.

When a tired parent orders delivery for the third time this week instead of making something at home, they are usually not buying a meal. They are buying a few minutes of relief from a hard day. That's not necessarily a bad purchase — but it's worth knowing that's what's actually being purchased.

Most spending decisions have a **real purchase underneath the stated one**. The product is just the vehicle. The actual destination is a feeling, an identity, a status, or a sense of being okay. And here's where it matters: the product almost always delivers the feeling briefly — and then it fades, and the same hunger comes back, usually a little stronger.

## Spending to Belong

This one is worth sitting with, because it's the most common driver of teen spending — and one of the most powerful forces in human psychology at any age.

The hunger to belong is not a flaw. It's fundamental. Humans are wired for group membership in a deep way, and for teenagers that wiring is especially intense. Being part of a group, fitting into a crowd, not being visibly different from the people around you — these feel like survival, because not too long ago in human history, they kind of were.

The problem is that marketers know this, and the feed knows this, and a whole machine is built around it. When a teen buys the right gear, the right shoes, the right phone case, the right brand, they're often buying a ticket to perceived belonging. They want to be accepted, included, and not marked as outside the group. That is a completely human desire.

But here's what's worth naming together: **real belonging doesn't send a bill later.** The people who actually like and accept your kid — not the version of your kid wearing the right shoes — don't require a purchase. When belonging is contingent on what you own, it's not belonging. It's a transaction.

That doesn't mean your kid has to go full minimalist to prove a point. But it means there's a question worth asking before the purchase: *if I had this thing and the people around me still didn't accept me, would I still want it?* The answer is often telling.

## Spending to Impress

There's a slightly different category worth naming separately: spending to impress people, even people your kid doesn't particularly like, or doesn't know well, or whose opinion they won't care about in five years.

This is so common that it's practically a default. People buy things for an audience — a social media audience, a lunch-table audience, an after-school audience. The mental transaction is: *if I have this, I'll be seen a certain way.* And the status hit is real — briefly. Then it fades. Then the bar has moved, and there's a next thing that would raise the perceived status again.

The honest version of this isn't shaming. It's just: most of us have done this. Bought something to impress people who weren't paying that close attention, or who were impressed for about a week, or who wouldn't have thought less of you if you hadn't bought it. The purchase filled a feeling for a moment and then the feeling came back empty.

When your kid can name "I want this partly because of how I think it'll make me look," they're not failing — they're being honest. And honest is exactly where good decisions get made.

## The Question That Changes Everything

There's one question worth teaching your kid to ask before any significant purchase — especially one that's driven by a want rather than a need:

**What am I actually trying to buy here?**

Not "why do I want this?" That's easy to deflect. But "what am I actually trying to buy?" forces a more honest look. Is it the item itself, with genuine utility? Or is it a feeling — confidence, comfort, status, belonging, relief? And if it's a feeling, is this item actually the best way to get it? Or is there something cheaper, or more lasting, or more real?

This isn't about making every purchase an existential inquiry. It's about building a reflex that slows the automatic spending down enough to make it a choice. Most of the time, the purchase is still fine. But sometimes the question reveals something important — that the real want is completely separate from the item, and maybe even the item can't deliver it.

## The Purchases That Actually Fill Something

Here's the other side of this, because it's not all grimness: some spending really does deliver something real and lasting. Paying for an experience with people you love. Buying something that enables a skill you're building. Spending on something that genuinely makes your life better in a durable way — not just for the first week you have it.

When your kid makes a purchase and they're still glad about it a month later, that's a good sign they bought something real. When the purchase fades into the background immediately — when the satisfaction was mostly in the anticipation and the unboxing — that's the kind of buying worth examining.

You're not trying to raise a kid who never spends on anything that brings them joy. You're trying to raise a kid who knows the difference between joy and the temporary hit of buying a feeling that the purchase can't actually sustain.

## Try this together

Pick one recent want from your kid's life — something they've asked for, saved up for, or spent money on recently. It doesn't have to be big. Then dig underneath it together.

Ask: if you already had this thing and it turned out not to change how you felt, what would be missing? What are you really hoping it does for you?

Listen without judgment. Name the real purchase together — is it comfort, belonging, status, confidence, something to show people? Don't moralize about it. Just make it visible.

Then ask the next question: is this the best way to actually get that thing? Or is there something else — a conversation, an experience, a relationship — that would deliver it more reliably?

You can do this for yourself too, with something you've been wanting. Model it. Let your kid see you doing the same honest audit and coming to a real conclusion, not a performance of virtue.

## Questions to sit with

**For you (the parent):**
- What have you bought to impress people whose opinion you realize now didn't really matter — and what would you do differently with that money?
- Where in your own spending do you notice you're really buying a feeling — and is the feeling actually being delivered?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- What's a purchase you've made recently that was really about fitting in or belonging — not the item itself?
- When you think about something you really want right now, what's the feeling underneath the want?
- Can you think of something you bought that you were excited about for about a week and then stopped thinking about? What does that tell you?
