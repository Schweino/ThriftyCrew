Your bank balance tells you how today is going. One page, once a year, tells you whether you're actually getting ahead.

The big idea: A personal balance sheet is a list of what you own and what you owe, and the difference is your net worth. Write it down once a year, watch which way it moves, and don't panic if it starts out negative. In your twenties, it usually does.

What a Personal Balance Sheet Is

Let's strip the fancy off this one. Businesses keep balance sheets, and there's nothing complicated about them. A personal balance sheet is the same tool, pointed at your own kitchen table.

Assets. Everything you own that's worth money. Cash, savings, retirement and investment accounts, what your car would sell for today, a home if you own one.

Liabilities. Everything you owe. Credit cards, student loans, a car loan, what's left on a mortgage, money you owe a person.

Net worth. Assets minus liabilities. One number.

That's it. Two lists and a minus sign.

Here's why it matters. Your paycheck tells you what comes in. Your budget tells you where it goes. But neither one tells you whether all those months are actually adding up to anything. This page does. Two people can earn the same paycheck for ten years and end up in completely different places, and this is the page where that gap shows up.

How to Calculate Your Net Worth

Here's the simple version. Four steps.

  1. List what you own, at what it would sell for today. Not what you paid. A car you bought for $20,000 a few years back is worth whatever somebody would hand you for it now. Look it up. Don't guess high.
  2. Skip the small stuff. The couch, the clothes, the kitchen table are technically assets, but they sell for next to nothing. Leave them off and the number stays honest.
  3. List everything you owe, at today's balance. Pull the real numbers off the statements or the lender's website. Don't round down.
  4. Subtract. What you own minus what you owe. Write the number and today's date at the top of the page.

Then put that page somewhere you'll find it next year. The date matters, because the second page is where this thing really starts earning its keep.

A Negative Number in Your Twenties Is Normal

This is the part I most want your kid to hear. So let's walk through an example. These numbers are made up to show the math, not a benchmark for anybody.

Picture a new college graduate. First job, first apartment.

What they own: $1,200 in checking, $800 in savings, and a used car that would sell for about $6,000. That's $8,000.

What they owe: $14,000 in student loans, $1,500 left on the car loan, and $500 on a credit card. That's $16,000.

Net worth: $8,000 minus $16,000. Negative $8,000.

That number can land like a punch in the gut. It shouldn't. This person just spent four years building the skills they'll earn a living with for the next forty, and the loan is what those skills cost. Negative at 22 isn't a verdict. It's a starting line.

Now jump ahead one year. Say they paid $3,000 off their debts and put another $500 in savings. To keep the math simple we'll hold the car's value flat, though in real life it would probably slip a little. Now they own $8,500 and owe $13,000. Net worth: negative $4,500.

Still negative. But $3,500 better in one year. That's the whole reason to write it down. On its own, negative $4,500 looks bad. Sitting next to last year's page, it looks like progress. Because it is. Celebrate that one.

Once a Year Is Plenty

You don't need to check this every week. Net worth moves slowly, and staring at it every day turns a planning tool into a worry machine. Once a year gives the small, boring choices time to show up. A little less spent than earned. A little less owed each month. A little more saved.

Pick a date you'll remember. A birthday works. So does the first week of January. Same date, same page, every year. Stack a few of those up and you'll have something most people never have: a record of your own climb.

Once you've got your number, you can see where it lands against the real middle of the country for your age in Net Worth by Age, which uses the Federal Reserve's own survey numbers. Just remember, a benchmark is a marker, not a scorecard. The comparison that matters most is you against last year's you.

This is general education, not financial advice. For decisions about your own money, talk to a qualified professional.

Try This Together

This is a one-evening worksheet for you and your teen. Each of you fills out your own. Your kid's will be small, and that's the point. The habit is the lesson, not the size of the numbers. If you'd rather keep your household numbers private, do yours on your own and just talk through the steps together.

Worksheet: My Balance Sheet, dated ____

What I own

  • Cash and checking: $____
  • Savings: $____
  • Investment or retirement accounts: $____
  • Car or other vehicle (what it would sell for today): $____
  • Other things worth real money (a phone, a bike, gear I could sell): $____
  • Total I own: $____

What I owe

  • Credit cards: $____
  • Car loan: $____
  • Student loans: $____
  • Money I owe a person (a parent, a sibling, a friend): $____
  • Anything else I owe: $____
  • Total I owe: $____

My net worth: Total I own minus Total I owe = $____

One thing I'll do this year to move it up: ____

When you're both done, fold the pages up and seal them in an envelope with next year's date on the front. A year from now, open it, fill out a new one, and compare. That envelope is one of the best gifts Future You will ever get.

Questions to Sit With

For yourself:

  • When's the last time you actually added up what you own and what you owe? Why has it been that long?
  • Which one line on your page would move your net worth the most if you worked on it this year?
  • Are you measuring yourself against last year's you, or against somebody else's highlight reel?

For you (the parent):

  • What did you believe about debt and net worth at your kid's age? How much of it turned out to be true?
  • How much of your own page are you comfortable sharing, and what would your kid learn from seeing it?

For your kid (ask them, or do it together):

  • If you added up everything you own that you could sell, what do you think it would come to?
  • Why might a new college grad start out with a negative net worth? Is that a bad thing?
  • What's one thing you could do before next year's envelope to push your number up?

Common questions

What is a personal balance sheet?A list of everything you own (assets) and everything you owe (liabilities). The difference between the two totals is your net worth.
How do I calculate my net worth?Add up what your assets would sell for today, add up the current balances of your debts, and subtract the debts from the assets.
Is a negative net worth bad?Not necessarily. It is common early in adult life, especially with student loans and few assets yet. What matters most is whether the number is moving up year over year.
