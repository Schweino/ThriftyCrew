The most important money in your house might be the money that never does anything. Here's how much should be in an emergency fund, and why it comes before investing.

The big idea: An emergency fund is cash set aside for the bills nobody schedules. How much you need comes down mostly to one question, how hard your income would be to replace. And it comes before investing, because it's what keeps you from ever having to sell at the worst possible time.

How Much Should Be in an Emergency Fund?

Here's the straight answer. Then the honest one.

The straight answer: the range you'll hear most from financial planners is three to six months of essential expenses. If your household runs on one income, or on income that would be hard to replace, a lot of them say six to twelve months.

The honest answer: that range is wide on purpose, and where you land in it depends on how replaceable your income is. Two steady paychecks in jobs that are easy to find again? The low end can work. One income, commission pay, self-employment, or a field where the next job could take a while to land? Aim higher. Anybody who hands you one exact number for everybody is quoting a default. Not your life.

Notice the word essential. This isn't three months of life the way you live it today. It's rent or the mortgage, groceries, utilities, insurance, getting to work, minimum debt payments. The stuff that has to get paid even in a bad month. Streaming and eating out can pause. The light bill can't.

Where Most Families Actually Stand

Every year the Federal Reserve asks American adults a simple question: if a $400 bill showed up out of nowhere, how would you pay it? In its report on 2025, published in May 2026, 63 percent said they'd cover it all with cash or something just as good. (The Fed counts a credit card paid off in full at the next statement as just as good.) That means 37 percent would need some other way to handle a $400 bill.

The same report found 55 percent of adults had three months of expenses set aside in an emergency or rainy-day fund.

Put those two numbers side by side and here's what they tell me. Nearly half of adults don't have that three-month cushion. And a $400 surprise isn't rare. It's a tire. It's a vet visit. It's the water heater picking a Sunday to quit. So if you don't have this fund yet, you're not behind some imaginary crowd. You're right in the middle of real life, and that's exactly where we start.

Why the Buffer Comes Before Investing

If you've gone through the custodial account conversation and index funds 101, you know I love starting early with investing. So why does this come first?

Because investments go up and down, and they don't check your calendar. The market can be down the exact week your car dies. If every dollar you've got is invested, you sell at whatever it's worth that day and lock in the loss. Or you put the repair on a credit card and pay interest on it for months.

An emergency fund breaks that chain. The surprise hits, you pay it in cash, the investments stay put, and time keeps doing its quiet work on them. The buffer isn't the opposite of investing. It's the thing that lets investing actually work.

That's also why this money doesn't go in the market. Its whole job is to be there, all of it, on the worst day. Keep it somewhere boring. Separate from your everyday checking, so it isn't tempting. Easy to reach in a day or two. A savings account at a federally insured bank or credit union does the job just fine.

Let's Put Real Numbers on It

Let's make it concrete with a made-up household. These numbers are an example, not a benchmark, so plug in your own.

Say the essentials come to $3,200 a month: $1,300 for rent, $700 for groceries, $250 for utilities and phones, $350 for the car and gas, $300 for insurance, and $300 in minimum debt payments.

If it's two steady incomes, both easy to replace, three months is $9,600 and six months is $19,200. If it's one income, or pay that swings with commission, six months is $19,200 and twelve months is $38,400.

That can feel like a mountain. So don't try to climb it in one day.

Your first stop is $1,000. It won't cover every disaster. But it covers a lot of the everyday ones, like a tire or a vet visit, the kind that usually end up on a credit card. Here's how to build it fast.

Say this family can set aside $400 a month. The first $1,000 takes two and a half months. One month of essentials, $3,200, takes 8 months. Three months, $9,600, takes 24 months. That's two years. But it's two years where every surprise along the way gets a little easier, because the fund is doing its job long before it's "done." Celebrate that first $1,000 like it's a big deal. Because it is.

And when you do have to use it, that's not failure. That's the fund working. Pay the bill, then refill it the same way you built it.

This is general education, not financial advice. Your right number depends on your own income, health, family and job, so talk it through with a qualified professional if you're not sure.

Try This Together

Grab last month's bank and card statements and sit down together. Three steps.

  1. Find the essentials. Circle only the must-pay bills and add them up. That's one month.
  2. Pick your number. Ask the one question that matters: If our main paycheck stopped tomorrow, how long would it honestly take to replace? A few weeks points you toward three months. A long search, one income or pay that bounces around points you toward six or more. Then let the emergency fund calculator do the multiplying, and show you how long it takes at what you can set aside each month.
  3. Give the money a home and a name. Open or rename a separate savings account. "Emergency Fund" works. "Do Not Touch" works better. Set up an automatic transfer on payday, even a small one.

Then run the same thing at your teen's size. What are their must-pays? A phone bill, gas, their share of the car insurance? One month of that is their first emergency fund. Building it teaches the whole idea in miniature, and it's a lot more fun to learn at $150 than at $15,000.

Questions to Sit With

For yourself:

  • If your income stopped tomorrow, how many months of essentials could you cover right now? Be honest.
  • How replaceable is your income, really? Does your target match that answer?
  • Think of one surprise bill from the last year. How would it have felt different with this fund already in place?

For you (the parent):

  • Does your kid know your family keeps money set aside for emergencies? Do they know why?
  • Would you rather they learn about a buffer from you now, or from their first surprise bill later?

For your kid (ask them, or do it together):

  • What's the most expensive "oops" that could happen to you this year? A cracked phone? A flat tire?
  • Why do you think emergency money should sit somewhere boring instead of being invested?
  • How many weeks of saving would it take you to cover that "oops" all the way?

Should I build an emergency fund before investing?The usual order is buffer first. Without one, a surprise bill can force you to sell investments at a bad time or borrow on a credit card.
