The most important money in your house might be the money that never does anything. Here's how much should be in an emergency fund, and why it comes before investing.

The big idea: An emergency fund is cash set aside for the bills nobody schedules. How much you need depends mostly on one thing, how hard your income would be to replace, and it comes before investing because it's what keeps you from ever having to sell at the worst time.

How much should be in an emergency fund?

Here's the straight answer, and then the honest one.

The straight answer: most financial planners quote three to six months of essential expenses. For a household that runs on a single income, or on income that would be hard to replace, many go to six to twelve months.

The honest answer: that range is wide on purpose, and the thing that moves you around inside it is how replaceable your income is. Two steady paychecks in jobs that are easy to find again? The lower end can work. One income, commission pay, self-employment, or a job in a field where the next one could take a while to land? Aim higher. Anybody who tells you one exact number for everyone is quoting a default, not your situation.

Notice the word essential. This is not three months of your whole life as it looks today. It's rent or the mortgage, groceries, utilities, insurance, transportation, minimum debt payments. The stuff that has to get paid even in a bad month. Streaming services and eating out can pause. The light bill can't.

Where most households actually stand

Every year the Federal Reserve asks American adults how they would pay a surprise $400 expense. In its report on 2025, published in May 2026, 63 percent said they'd cover it entirely with cash or its equivalent, which the Fed defines as cash, savings, or a credit card paid off at the next statement (source: Federal Reserve, Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in 2025). That leaves 37 percent who would need some other way to handle a $400 bill.

The same report found 55 percent of adults said they had set aside three months of expenses in an emergency or rainy-day fund.

Read those two numbers together and here's what they tell me. Nearly half of adults don't have the three-month cushion, and a $400 surprise isn't a rare event. It's a tire. It's a vet visit. It's the water heater picking a Sunday to quit. If you don't have this fund yet, you're not behind some imaginary crowd. You're right in the middle of real life, and that's exactly where we start.

Why the buffer comes before investing

If you've been through our lessons on the custodial account and index funds, you know I love starting early with investing. So why does this come first?

Because investments go up and down, and they don't check your calendar first. The stock market can be down on the exact week your car dies. If the only money you have is invested, you sell whatever it's worth that day, and you lock in the loss. Or you put the repair on a credit card and pay interest on it for months.

An emergency fund breaks that chain. When the surprise hits, you pay it from cash, the investments stay put, and time keeps doing its quiet work on them. The buffer isn't the opposite of investing. It's the thing that lets investing actually work.

That's also why the emergency fund doesn't live in the market. Its job is to be there, in full, on the worst day. Keep it somewhere boring, separate from your everyday checking so it isn't tempting, and easy to reach within a day or two. A separate savings account at a federally insured bank or credit union does that job.

A worked example

Let's make it real with a made-up household. These numbers are an example, not a benchmark. Plug in your own.

Say the essentials come to $3,200 a month: $1,300 for rent, $700 for groceries, $250 for utilities and phones, $350 for car costs and gas, $300 for insurance, and $300 in minimum debt payments.

  • Two steady incomes, both easy to replace: three months is $9,600, six months is $19,200.
  • One income, or pay that swings with commission: six months is $19,200, twelve months is $38,400.

Those numbers can feel like a mountain. So don't climb it all at once.

Suppose this household can set aside $400 a month. One month of essentials, $3,200, takes 8 months. Three months, $9,600, takes 24 months. That's two years, and it's two years where every single surprise along the way gets easier, because the fund is doing its job long before it's "done." Every step counts. Celebrate the first month like it's a big deal, because it is.

One more thing: when you use the fund, that's not failure. That's the fund working. You pay the bill, then you refill it, the same way you built it.

This is general education, not financial advice. Your right number depends on your own income, health, family and job, so talk it through with a qualified professional if you're unsure.

Try this together

Sit down with last month's bank and card statements and do three things:

  1. Find your essentials. Circle only the must-pay bills and add them up. That's one month.
  2. Pick your multiplier. Ask the one question that matters: If our main income stopped tomorrow, how long would it realistically take to replace? Weeks points you toward three months. A long search, one income or unsteady pay points you toward six or more.
  3. Give the money a home and a name. Open or rename a separate savings account. "Emergency Fund" works. "Do Not Touch" works better. Set an automatic transfer on payday, even a small one.

For a teen, run the same exercise at their scale. What are their must-pays: a phone bill, gas, a car insurance share? One month of that is their first emergency fund, and building it teaches the whole idea in miniature.

Questions to sit with

For yourself:

  • If my income stopped tomorrow, how many months could I cover the essentials right now?
  • How replaceable is my income, honestly? Does my target match that answer?
  • What's one surprise bill from the last year that would have felt different with this fund in place?

For you (the parent):

  • Does your kid know your family keeps money set aside for emergencies, and why?
  • Would you rather they learn about a buffer from you now, or from their first surprise bill later?

For your kid (ask them, or do it together):

  • What's the most expensive "oops" that could happen to you this year? A cracked phone? A flat tire?
  • Why do you think it's smart to keep emergency money somewhere boring instead of investing it?
  • How many weeks of saving would it take you to cover that "oops" in full?
