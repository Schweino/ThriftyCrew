# The One Page That Shows Where You Really Stand

*Your bank balance tells you how today is going. One page, once a year, tells you whether you're actually getting ahead.*

**The big idea:** A personal balance sheet is a list of what you own and what you owe, and the difference is your net worth. Write it once a year, watch which way it moves, and don't panic if it starts out negative. In your twenties, it usually does.

## What a personal balance sheet is

Businesses keep balance sheets, and there's nothing fancy about them. A **personal balance sheet** is the same tool, pointed at your household:

- **Assets:** everything you own that's worth money. Cash, savings, retirement and investment accounts, what your car would sell for today, the value of a home if you own one.
- **Liabilities:** everything you owe. Credit card balances, student loans, a car loan, what's left on a mortgage, money you owe a person.
- **Net worth:** assets minus liabilities. One number.

That's it. Two lists and a subtraction sign.

Here's why it matters. Income tells you how much comes in. A budget tells you where it goes. But neither one tells you whether all those months are adding up to anything. The balance sheet does. Two people can earn the same paycheck for ten years and end up in completely different places, and this page is where that gap shows up.

## How to calculate your net worth, step by step

1. **List what you own, at what it would sell for today.** Not what you paid. A car bought for $20,000 a few years ago is worth whatever someone would hand you for it now. Look it up rather than guessing high.
2. **Skip the small stuff.** Your couch, your clothes and your kitchen table are technically assets, but they sell for very little. Leave them off and the number stays honest.
3. **List everything you owe, at today's balance.** Pull the real balances from the statements or the lender's website. Don't round down.
4. **Subtract.** Assets minus liabilities. Write the number and the date at the top of the page.

Then put the page somewhere you'll find it next year. That date matters, because the second balance sheet is where this tool starts earning its keep.

## A negative number in your twenties is normal

This is the part I most want a young person to hear, so let's walk through it with an example. These numbers are made up to show the arithmetic, not a benchmark for anybody.

Picture a new graduate, first job, first apartment.

**What they own:** $1,200 in checking, $800 in savings, and a used car that would sell for about $6,000. That's **$8,000** in assets.

**What they owe:** $14,000 in student loans, $1,500 left on the car loan, and $500 on a credit card. That's **$16,000** in liabilities.

**Net worth:** $8,000 minus $16,000 is **negative $8,000**.

That number can land like a punch in the stomach. It shouldn't. This person just spent four years building the skills that will earn their living for the next forty, and the loan is what those skills cost. Negative at 22 isn't a verdict. It's a starting line.

Now fast-forward one year. Say that over the year they paid $3,000 off their debts and added $500 to savings. To keep the arithmetic simple we'll hold the car's value flat, though in real life it would probably drop a bit. Assets are now $8,500 and liabilities are $13,000. Net worth: **negative $4,500**.

Still negative. And a $3,500 improvement in one year. That's the whole point of writing it down. On a single day, negative $4,500 looks bad. Next to last year's page, it looks like progress, because it is. Celebrate that.

## Once a year is enough

You don't need to check this every week. Net worth moves slowly, and staring at it daily turns a planning tool into a worry machine. Once a year gives the small, boring choices time to show up: a little less spent than earned, a little less owed each month, a little more saved.

Pick a date you'll remember. A birthday works. So does the first week of January. Same date every year, same page, and over time you'll have something most people never have: a record of your own climb.

Once you have your number, you can see how it compares with the real middle of the country for your age in our [Net Worth by Age](/net-worth-by-age/) lesson, which uses the Federal Reserve's survey data. Just remember that a benchmark is a marker, not a scorecard. The comparison that matters most is you against last year's you.

*This is general education, not financial advice. For decisions about your own money, talk to a qualified professional.*

## Try this together

This is a one-evening worksheet for a parent and a teen. Each of you fills out your own. A teen's version will be small, and that's the point: the habit is the lesson, not the size of the numbers. If you'd rather keep your household numbers private, do yours on your own and just talk through the steps together.

**Worksheet: My Balance Sheet, dated ____**

**What I own (assets)**
- Cash and checking: $____
- Savings: $____
- Investment or retirement accounts: $____
- Car or other vehicle (what it would sell for today): $____
- Other things worth real money (a phone, a bike, gear you could sell): $____
- **Total I own:** $____

**What I owe (liabilities)**
- Credit cards: $____
- Car loan: $____
- Student loans: $____
- Money I owe a person (a parent, a sibling, a friend): $____
- Anything else I owe: $____
- **Total I owe:** $____

**My net worth:** Total I own minus Total I owe = $____

**One thing I'll do this year to move it up:** ____

When you're both done, fold the pages up and put them in an envelope with next year's date on it. A year from now, open it, fill out a new one, and compare. That envelope is one of the best gifts Future You will ever get.

## Questions to sit with

**For yourself:**
- When was the last time I actually added up what I own and what I owe, and why has it been that long?
- Which one line on my balance sheet would change my net worth the most if I worked on it this year?
- Am I measuring myself against last year's me, or against somebody else's highlight reel?

**For you (the parent):**
- What did you believe about debt and net worth at your kid's age, and how much of it turned out to be true?
- How much of your own balance sheet are you comfortable sharing, and what would your kid learn from seeing it?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- If you added up everything you own that you could sell, what do you think it would come to?
- Why do you think a new college graduate might start out with a negative net worth, and is that a bad thing?
- What's one thing you could do before next year's envelope to make your number go up?
