# What You're Really Buying When You Buy Insurance

*Insurance is the one bill you hope you never get your money's worth out of. Here's how to buy it like you know that.*

**The big idea:** Insurance trades a small, certain cost now for protection against a big, uncertain one later. Learn three words, buy the coverage life hands you first, and save your insurance dollars for the disasters you could never pay for yourself.

## Insurance basics: three words that do most of the work

Insurance basics come down to three words. Learn them once and every policy you ever read gets easier.

- **Policy.** The contract. It says what's covered, what isn't, and the most the insurance company will pay.
- **Premium.** What you pay to keep the policy, monthly or every six months or once a year. You pay it whether anything goes wrong or not.
- **Deductible.** The part of a claim you pay yourself before the insurance company pays anything.

Here's how those fit together, with made-up numbers for the arithmetic. Say your car policy has a $500 deductible and a fender-bender does $2,000 of damage to your car. You pay the first $500. The insurance company pays the other $1,500.

Now the trade most people never notice. A higher deductible usually means a lower premium, and a lower deductible means a higher one. So the real question isn't "which deductible is cheapest?" It's "what's the biggest bill I could actually pay tomorrow without borrowing?" If the honest answer is $300, a $1,000 deductible is a trapdoor, not a savings. That's why this lesson and [the emergency fund lesson](/how-much-emergency-fund/) are really one idea: the deductible you can choose depends on the cash you already have set aside.

## The first policy most of us buy: auto

For a lot of families, auto insurance is the first policy a young person ever touches, usually the day they get their license and join the family policy.

According to the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC), the regulators' own association, most states require you to carry a minimum amount of **liability** coverage, and the amount varies by state ([source](https://content.naic.org/article/what-you-should-know-about-auto-insurance-coverage)). Liability is the part that pays for damage and injuries you cause to *other people*. It protects them from you, and it protects your future paychecks from a lawsuit.

Two more pieces show up on most policies:

- **Collision** pays to fix your own car after you hit something. The NAIC notes it isn't required by law, but a lender may require it if there's a loan on the car.
- **Comprehensive** pays for damage from almost everything else: theft, fire, hail, vandalism, a cracked windshield. Also optional, unless a lender says otherwise.

Here's the parent move I'd make. Call your agent *before* your teen gets their license, not after. Ask what adding them will do to the premium, and ask what would bring it down. Then share that number with your kid. Nothing teaches "driving has a cost" like seeing the real line on the real bill.

## Renter's insurance: what it covers that the landlord's doesn't

This one surprises people, and it usually shows up with the first apartment.

Your landlord almost certainly has insurance. But per the NAIC, the landlord's coverage takes care of damage to the **building's structure**. It doesn't cover your stuff ([source](https://content.naic.org/article/consumer-insight-rent-protecting-your-belongings-renters-insurance)). If a pipe bursts and ruins your laptop, your bed and your clothes, the landlord's policy fixes the walls. Your things are on you.

That's the gap renter's insurance fills. The NAIC describes two basic parts, plus a third on some policies:

- **Personal property:** pays to repair or replace your belongings if they're damaged, destroyed or stolen.
- **Liability:** covers you if someone is hurt in your place, or if you accidentally damage someone else's property.
- **Additional living expenses:** some policies also pay for somewhere to stay if your apartment becomes unlivable.

One detail worth asking about when you shop: **actual cash value** versus **replacement cost**. Actual cash value pays what your old stuff was worth at the time of the claim, after wear and age. Replacement cost pays what it takes to buy new, usually after you've bought it and sent in the receipts ([source](https://content.naic.org/article/consumer-insight-renters-insurance-purchase-or-not-purchase)). A five-year-old couch is worth a lot less than a new one, so that difference is real money.

## Life insurance: term versus permanent

Life insurance pays money to the people you name if you die while the policy is in force. The question to ask first isn't "which kind?" It's "does anyone depend on my income?" A teenager with no dependents usually doesn't need it yet. A parent with kids and a mortgage usually does.

There are two broad families, and the NAIC lays them out plainly ([source](https://content.naic.org/article/consumer-insight-what-type-life-insurance-right-you)):

- **Term life** covers you for a set number of years. It's designed to be the lower-cost way to get coverage, and most term policies build no cash value. If you renew when the term ends, the premium may be higher than the original.
- **Permanent life** (whole, universal and variable are the common types) can last your whole life and carries a savings or investment piece, called cash value. That's a big part of why it costs more for the same death benefit.

You'll notice I haven't put a price on either. That's on purpose. A life insurance premium depends on your age, your health, how much coverage you buy and for how long, so any single number you read online is somebody else's quote, not yours. The only price that matters is the one you get when you ask. What *is* true for almost everyone is the shape: coverage bought younger and healthier tends to cost less than the same coverage bought later.

## The rule underneath all of it

Here's the idea that ties every policy together. **Insure the disasters. Pay for the dents yourself.**

A house fire, a lawsuit after a car accident, a family losing its main income: those are the bills that can wreck a household for years, and that's exactly what insurance is built for. A cracked phone screen isn't. If you could replace something without borrowing, paying a premium to protect it every month is often the expensive way to go. The more you have in savings, the more small stuff you can cover yourself, and the more of your premium goes toward the big stuff that actually matters.

That's the whole game. Pay a little now, on purpose, so one bad day never costs Future You a lot later.

*This is general education, not financial or insurance advice. Coverage rules vary by state and by policy, so check the details with a licensed agent or your state's insurance department.*

## Try this together

Pull out one real policy you already have, auto or renter's. Find the page near the front that summarizes your coverage (insurers usually call it the **declarations page**). Together, find and write down four things:

1. The **premium**, and how often you pay it.
2. The **deductible** for each coverage.
3. The **liability limits**, the most the policy will pay for damage you cause to others.
4. One thing the policy says it does **not** cover.

Then ask one question out loud: *If we had to pay that deductible tomorrow, could we do it without borrowing?* If the answer is yes, celebrate that. If it's no, you've just found the first thing to fix, and it isn't the insurance.

For a teen: walk around their room with a phone and record a slow video of everything they own. That's a home inventory, the list you'd want in hand if you ever had to file a claim, and it only takes a few minutes.

## Questions to sit with

**For yourself:**
- Do I know my deductibles off the top of my head, and could I pay the biggest one tomorrow?
- Am I paying to insure anything small I could easily replace myself?
- If someone depends on my income, have I actually asked for a life insurance quote, or just wondered about it?

**For you (the parent):**
- What will adding your teen as a driver do to your premium, and have you asked yet?
- When your kid moves into their first place, how will you make sure renter's insurance is on the checklist?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- If our car got scratched in a parking lot, who do you think pays for it, and how much?
- What would it cost to replace everything in your room? Let's guess, then add it up.
- Why do you think someone would pay for insurance every month for years and hope they never use it?
