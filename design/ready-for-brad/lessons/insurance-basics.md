# What You're Really Buying When You Buy Insurance

*Insurance is the one bill you hope you never get your money's worth out of. Here's how to buy it like you know that.*

**The big idea:** Insurance is trading a small bill you know about for protection from a big one you don't. Learn three words, buy the coverage life hands you first, and save your insurance money for the disasters you could never pay for yourself.

## Three Words That Do Most of the Work

Insurance basics come down to three words. Learn them once and every policy you ever read gets a whole lot easier.

**Policy.** The contract. It says what's covered, what isn't, and the most the insurance company will pay.

**Premium.** What you pay to keep the policy. Monthly, every six months, or once a year. You pay it whether anything goes wrong or not.

**Deductible.** The part of a claim you pay yourself before the insurance company pays a dime.

Here's the simple version, with made-up numbers. Say your car policy has a $500 deductible, and a fender-bender does $2,000 of damage to your car. You pay the first $500. The insurance company pays the other $1,500.

Now here's the part most folks never think about. A higher deductible usually means a lower premium. A lower deductible means a higher one. So the real question isn't "which one is cheapest?" It's "what's the biggest bill I could pay tomorrow without borrowing?" If the honest answer is $300, a $1,000 deductible isn't a savings. It's a trapdoor. That's why this lesson and [the emergency fund lesson](/how-much-emergency-fund/) are really one idea. The deductible you can live with depends on the cash you've already got set aside.

## The First Policy Your Teen Will Touch: Auto

For a lot of families, car insurance is the first policy a kid ever deals with. Usually it's the day they get their license and join yours.

Here's what you're paying for. The part called **liability** pays for damage and injuries you cause to *other people*. It protects them from you, and it protects your future paychecks from a lawsuit. The [National Association of Insurance Commissioners](https://content.naic.org/article/what-you-should-know-about-auto-insurance-coverage), the group made up of each state's insurance regulators, says most states require a minimum amount of it, and the amount varies by state.

Two more pieces show up on most policies. **Collision** pays to fix your own car after you hit something. **Comprehensive** pays for pretty much everything else: theft, fire, hail, vandalism, a cracked windshield. Neither one is required by law. But if there's a loan on the car, the lender may require them.

Here's the parent move I'd make. Call your agent *before* your teen gets their license, not after. Ask what adding them will do to the premium, and ask what would bring it down. Then show your kid that number. Nothing teaches "driving costs money" like the real line on the real bill.

## Renter's Insurance: The Landlord's Policy Doesn't Cover Your Stuff

This one catches people off guard, and it usually shows up with the first apartment.

Your kid's landlord almost certainly has insurance. But the NAIC is clear about what it covers: [the building's structure](https://content.naic.org/article/consumer-insight-rent-protecting-your-belongings-renters-insurance). Not your stuff. If a pipe bursts and ruins the laptop, the bed and the clothes, the landlord's policy fixes the walls. Everything else is on you.

That's the gap renter's insurance fills. It does two main jobs, plus a third on some policies:

- **Your things.** It pays to repair or replace your belongings if they're damaged, destroyed or stolen.
- **Liability.** It covers you if someone gets hurt at your place, or if you accidentally damage someone else's property.
- **A place to stay.** Some policies also pay your living costs if the apartment becomes unlivable.

One question worth asking when you shop: does it pay **actual cash value** or **replacement cost**? Actual cash value pays what your old stuff was worth at the time, after wear and tear. [Replacement cost](https://content.naic.org/article/consumer-insight-renters-insurance-purchase-or-not-purchase) pays what it takes to buy it new, usually after you buy it and send in the receipts. A five-year-old couch is worth a lot less than a new one. That difference is real money.

## Life Insurance: Term vs. Permanent

Life insurance pays money to the people you name if you die while the policy is in force. The first question isn't "which kind?" It's "does anybody depend on my income?" A teenager with no one depending on them usually doesn't need it yet. A parent with kids and a mortgage usually does.

There are two broad types, and the [NAIC lays them out](https://content.naic.org/article/consumer-insight-what-type-life-insurance-right-you) plainly.

**Term life** covers you for a set number of years. It's meant to be the lower-cost way to get coverage, and most term policies don't build any cash value. If you renew when the term runs out, the premium may be higher than it was the first time.

**Permanent life** (whole, universal and variable are the common kinds) can last your whole life, and it carries a savings or investment piece called cash value. That's a big part of why it costs more for the same payout.

You'll notice I didn't put a price on either one. That's on purpose. What you pay depends on your age, your health, how much coverage you buy and for how long. Any number you read online is somebody else's quote, not yours. The only price that matters is the one you get when you ask. What *is* true for almost everybody is the shape of it: the same coverage tends to cost less when you buy it younger and healthier. Time is on your side here too.

## Insure the Disasters, Pay for the Dents

Here's the idea underneath every policy you'll ever buy.

A house fire. A lawsuit after a car accident. A family losing the paycheck it runs on. Those are the bills that can wreck a household for years, and that's exactly what insurance is built for. A cracked phone screen isn't.

If you could replace something without borrowing, paying a premium every month to protect it is usually the expensive way to go. And the more you've got in savings, the more of the small stuff you can cover yourself. That frees up your insurance dollars for the big stuff that actually matters.

That's the whole game. Pay a little now, on purpose, so one bad day never costs Future You a lot later.

*This is general education, not financial or insurance advice. Coverage rules vary by state and by policy, so check the details with a licensed agent or your state's insurance department.*

## Try This Together

Pull out one real policy you already have. Auto or renter's, either one. Find the summary page near the front (insurers usually call it the **declarations page**). Then sit down with your kid and find four things:

1. The **premium**, and how often you pay it.
2. The **deductible** for each kind of coverage.
3. The **liability limits**, the most the policy will pay for damage you cause to someone else. They're often written as three numbers, like 100/300/100. [Here's how to read them](/liability-coverage/).
4. One thing the policy says it does **not** cover.

Then ask one question out loud: *If we had to pay that deductible tomorrow, could we do it without borrowing?* If the answer is yes, celebrate that. It's a real win. If it's no, you just found the first thing to fix, and it isn't the insurance.

One more for your teen: have them walk around their room with a phone and record a slow video of everything in it. That's a home inventory, the list you'd want in hand if you ever had to file a claim. It only takes a few minutes, and it turns "what's my stuff worth?" into something they can actually see.

## Questions to Sit With

**For yourself:**
- Do you know your deductibles off the top of your head? Could you pay the biggest one tomorrow without borrowing?
- Are you paying every month to insure something small you could easily replace yourself?
- If someone depends on your income, have you actually asked for a life insurance quote, or just thought about it?

**For you (the parent):**
- What will adding your teen as a driver do to your premium? Have you asked yet?
- When your kid moves into their first place, how will you make sure renter's insurance is on the checklist?

**For your kid (ask them, or do it together):**
- If our car got dinged in a parking lot, who do you think pays to fix it? How much?
- What would it cost to replace everything in your room? Take a guess, then let's add it up.
- Why would somebody pay for insurance every month for years and hope they never use it?
