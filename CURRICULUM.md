# Thrifty Crew — 52-Week Curriculum (Master Spec)

A year of weekly lessons for parents teaching teens **money + setting them up for success**.
Drawn from the book's frameworks, with all recovery/addiction content removed (see STYLE-GUIDE.md).

Sequenced to roughly follow a teen's year: **summer** = first jobs & money basics →
**fall** = work, school, reputation → **winter** = growing money & avoiding debt traps →
**spring** = character, choices & planning the road ahead.

Each writer: follow STYLE-GUIDE.md exactly. Filename = `lesson-NN-slug.md` (zero-padded, e.g.
`lesson-01-future-you-is-a-real-person.md`). Each lesson's header is `# Week N — Title`.

---

## QUARTER 1 — FOUNDATIONS: Mindset & Money Basics (Weeks 1–13)

**Week 1 — Future You Is a Real Person** · slug: `future-you-is-a-real-person`
Big idea: The single most powerful parenting lens for money/success — teach your kid that the
adult they become is a real person whose life is built by today's small choices.
Teach: most life-shaping decisions don't look big, they look like nothing; the question "will
Future Me thank me or pay for this?"; how to make "future you" feel real to a teen (it feels like
a cartoon to them). Frame the whole year ahead.
Try this together: each pick an age (e.g. 30) and write a short paragraph describing the life you
hope "Future You / Future Them" is living. Keep it; you'll revisit at Week 52.
Questions: parent — what did you wish you'd started earlier? kid — who do you want to be at 30?

**Week 2 — The Compounding Secret** · slug: `the-compounding-secret`
Big idea: Small things repeated don't add up — they multiply. The closest thing to a cheat code.
Teach: money compounds, but so do skills, habits, and reputation; compounding is invisible while
it works (that's why people quit); "stacking" small wins. Keep it concrete with a simple example.
Try this together: pick one tiny daily action each (10 min reading, $1/day saved, 10 pushups) and
track it on a visible chart for the week.
Questions: parent — what's one habit you've compounded over years? kid — what's one small thing
you could do daily that "future you" would thank you for?

**Week 3 — Win the First Five Minutes** · slug: `win-the-first-five-minutes`
Big idea: Start every day by accomplishing one thing; consistency builds a kid who follows through.
Teach: the magic isn't the task, it's the proof to yourself that "I'm someone who follows
through"; how this builds discipline that spills into money and work; parents modeling it.
Try this together: family "one morning thing" challenge for 7 days; put it on the fridge.
Questions: parent — what's your morning "one thing"? kid — what will yours be, and when?

**Week 4 — The Gap Is the Score** · slug: `the-gap-is-the-score`
Big idea: Money in, minus money out, equals what you keep. The gap — not income — is the score.
Teach: why a modest earner who keeps some can beat a big earner who spends it all; income is not
the score; the gap is the only money that ever builds anything. Make it vivid for a teen's
allowance/first paycheck.
Try this together: with any money your kid has, calculate their current "gap" (what came in vs.
went out last month). No judgment — just see the number.
Questions: parent — is your household focused on earning more or keeping more? kid — what's your
gap right now, and what's one way to widen it?

**Week 5 — Needs First, Then Wants** · slug: `needs-first-then-wants`
Big idea: Take care of what you NEED first, then *plan* when the fun happens.
Teach: the brain disguises wants as needs ("I need a night out / those shoes"); name the
difference; plan fun on purpose instead of impulse; this one rule prevents most money trouble.
Try this together: list your kid's last 10 purchases; sort each into NEED or WANT together (it
gets funny/revealing). No shame — awareness is the win.
Questions: parent — where do you treat a want like a need? kid — what's a want you've been calling
a need?

**Week 6 — Your Kid's First Budget** · slug: `your-kids-first-budget`
Big idea: A budget isn't a punishment — it's giving every dollar a job before it arrives.
Teach: how to build a dead-simple budget (in/out/left); the "money has a job before it shows up"
mindset; keep it boring and visible; planning a few paychecks/allowances ahead.
Try this together: build a one-page budget for your kid's actual money (allowance, job, gifts) —
3 buckets is enough: Save / Needs / Fun.
Questions: parent — do you budget, and would your kid know it? kid — what 3 buckets fit your money?

**Week 7 — Where Does the Money Go?** · slug: `where-does-the-money-go`
Big idea: You can't manage what you don't watch. Tracking beats guessing.
Teach: the difference between "I think I spent…" and knowing; simple tracking methods for a teen
(notes app, envelope, a shared sheet); the small-leaks insight (subscriptions, snacks, app buys).
Try this together: a 7-day spending log — every dollar, parent and kid both, then compare notes.
Questions: parent — what leak surprised you? kid — where did your money actually go this week?

**Week 8 — The First Job Conversation** · slug: `the-first-job-conversation`
Big idea: A first job isn't about the money — it's where a kid learns how money behaves and builds
their first references.
Teach: the real value of a first job (handling a paycheck, responsibility, references); jobs vs.
hustles (mowing, babysitting, tutoring); how to encourage without forcing; readiness signs.
Try this together: brainstorm 5 realistic first-job/hustle options that fit your kid's age and
interests; pick one to explore this month.
Questions: parent — what did your first job teach you that wasn't about pay? kid — which option
sounds least terrible (start there)?

**Week 9 — Pay Yourself First** · slug: `pay-yourself-first`
Big idea: Save a slice off the top, before anything else — automatically, every time.
Teach: why "save what's left" fails (nothing's left); the off-the-top habit; even 10% builds the
muscle; automate it; the saver's identity.
Try this together: agree on a "pay yourself first" percentage for your kid's next bit of income;
physically move it the day it arrives.
Questions: parent — do you pay yourself first? kid — what % can you commit to off the top?

**Week 10 — Opening the First Account** · slug: `opening-the-first-account`
Big idea: A real bank account turns abstract money lessons into hands-on practice.
Teach: checking vs. savings; debit cards (and how they differ from credit); teen/youth accounts;
reading a statement; safety basics. Practical walkthrough for parents.
Try this together: open (or review) a youth checking + savings account together; set up the app;
do one transfer to "savings" so they feel it.
Questions: parent — what account setup would've helped you at 16? kid — what's the difference
between your checking and savings for?

**Week 11 — Allowance vs. Earning** · slug: `allowance-vs-earning`
Big idea: How you structure money at home teaches a lesson either way — choose it on purpose.
Teach: pure allowance vs. commission/earned vs. hybrid; pros/cons of each; tying some money to
effort without turning every chore into a transaction; consistency matters more than the model.
Try this together: design (or revise) your family's money model on one page — what's given, what's
earned, what's expected for free (being part of the family).
Questions: parent — what's your current model teaching, intentionally or not? kid — what feels fair
to you, and why?

**Week 12 — Talking About Money Without Fighting** · slug: `talking-about-money-without-fighting`
Big idea: The family money culture your kid absorbs matters more than any single lesson.
Teach: why money is tense (it carries fear/shame); how to make money talk normal and calm; age-
appropriate transparency (what to share, what not to); modeling over lecturing; avoiding the
"money is scarce/scary" script.
Try this together: start a monthly 20-minute "Family Money Night" — low-key, snacks, no lectures.
Plan the first one.
Questions: parent — what money "vibe" did you inherit from your own parents? kid — how does money
feel to you — stressful, boring, exciting?

**Week 13 — Quarter Review + Family Money Night** · slug: `quarter-review-family-money-night`
Big idea: Count the victories. Celebrating wins is the fuel that keeps the habits going.
Teach: recap Q1 (Future You, gap, needs-first, budget, tracking, first job, pay-yourself-first);
why celebrating small wins beats grading against checkpoints; how to make progress visible.
Try this together: hold the first real Family Money Night — review the quarter, name 3 wins each,
celebrate them (a treat, a small reward).
Questions: parent — what's one win you didn't expect? kid — what's one money habit that's stuck?

## QUARTER 2 — EARNING, WORK & REPUTATION (Weeks 14–26)

**Week 14 — No One Is Coming to Save You (and That's Good News)** · slug: `ownership-and-responsibility`
Big idea: Help is real and worth taking — but the work at the center of your kid's life has their
name on it. Ownership is empowering, not cold.
Teach: the difference between support and rescue; "the situation may not be your fault, but the
response is your responsibility"; how blaming circumstances hands away your power; teaching kids to
own outcomes without crushing them. (Keep warm — this is about empowerment.)
Try this together: pick one small thing your kid has been blaming on others/circumstances; help
them name the one part *they* control and one action they'll take.
Questions: parent — where do you model ownership vs. blame? kid — what's one thing you can own this
week instead of waiting on someone else?

**Week 15 — Skills Beat Luck** · slug: `skills-beat-luck`
Big idea: Success people call "luck" is usually a stacked skill. Skill is buildable; luck isn't.
Teach: "lucky" is the comfortable story; the real engine is determination + time (not talent);
"some kids have it easier — so what, run your own race"; skills compound.
Try this together: name one skill your kid admires in someone; map the (probably invisible) hours
behind it. Reframe "they're lucky/talented" → "they practiced."
Questions: parent — what skill did you build that opened doors? kid — what skill, if you had it in
3 years, would change your options?

**Week 16 — Find the Thing That Pulls at You** · slug: `find-the-thing-that-pulls-at-you`
Big idea: Don't chase "hot" skills — chase genuine interest. Interest is the fuel that outlasts
motivation.
Teach: why interest beats prestige (forced skills fizzle); any interest counts (cars, art, code,
cooking, sports); parents' job is to notice and resource the spark, not assign it.
Try this together: help your kid list 3 things they'd happily learn even if no one paid them; pick
one to spend 2 hours on this week.
Questions: parent — are you nudging your kid toward your dream or theirs? kid — what would you learn
just because you want to?

**Week 17 — The Internet Is a Tool, Not a Trap** · slug: `the-internet-is-a-tool-not-a-trap`
Big idea: The same phone that can teach a kid anything can also vacuum up years. The tool doesn't
decide — the user does.
Teach: the greatest free library in history is in their pocket; the double edge (learn vs. endless
scroll); helping a kid point the tool at something instead of being used by it; not anti-tech,
pro-intention.
Try this together: audit one week of screen time together; pick one "consume" hour to convert into
a "create/learn" hour.
Questions: parent — what does your own phone use model? kid — what's one thing you could learn
online with time you usually scroll?

**Week 18 — Apply It or Lose It** · slug: `apply-it-or-lose-it`
Big idea: Watching a tutorial isn't learning. Knowledge you don't use evaporates; knowledge you
apply becomes a skill.
Teach: the trap of "feeling productive" by consuming; the power of building/fixing/practicing for
real; the confidence that comes from "I made that."
Try this together: whatever your kid is learning, have them produce one tiny real thing this week
(a dish, a drawing, a line of code, a fixed bike) and show it off.
Questions: parent — where do you consume without applying? kid — what did you make this week?

**Week 19 — Do the Work Before You're Paid for It** · slug: `do-the-work-before-youre-paid-for-it`
Big idea: The world is value-first, not pay-first. Become valuable, and the reward comes to find
you — usually later than you'd like.
Teach: most people wait to be rewarded before giving their best (backwards); the gap between work
and reward is where people quit; applies to first jobs AND schoolwork; the long game.
Try this together: find one place your kid can add value beyond what's required (extra effort at a
job, helping a teacher/neighbor) with no immediate payoff — and notice what it builds.
Questions: parent — when did doing more than required pay off later for you? kid — where could you
give more than the minimum this week?

**Week 20 — Your Reputation Travels Faster Than You Do** · slug: `your-reputation-travels`
Big idea: Long before your kid walks into a room, their reputation is already in the chair. It's
built one action at a time and it opens (or closes) doors they'll never see.
Teach: reputation = the sum of daily behavior stored in others' memories; "do what you say, 100%
of the time" is rare and valuable; it compounds; it's already forming at their age.
Try this together: talk about what your kid is currently "known for" (at school, on a team, at
work) — and one thing they'd want to be known for. Name one action that builds it.
Questions: parent — what are you known for, and is it on purpose? kid — what do you want your name
to mean to people?

**Week 21 — Show Up On Time, Every Time** · slug: `show-up-on-time-every-time`
Big idea: Reliability is a superpower precisely because it's rare. Doing what you said, when you
said, sets a kid apart instantly.
Teach: punctuality and follow-through as the cheapest competitive edge; how unreliability quietly
destroys trust; building the habit young (school, practice, jobs, promises at home).
Try this together: pick one commitment your kid will honor 100% this week (on time, every time);
parent picks one too. Compare at week's end.
Questions: parent — where do you let "mostly reliable" slide? kid — what's one promise you'll keep
perfectly this week?

**Week 22 — How to Get a First Job** · slug: `how-to-get-a-first-job`
Big idea: Demystify the mechanics — a teen can land a first job with a simple résumé, a few
applications, and basic preparation.
Teach: a first résumé with no experience (skills, school, volunteering, references); where to apply;
filling out applications well; how to ask an adult for a reference. Practical, step-by-step.
Try this together: draft a one-page résumé for your kid this week (template-style). Even if they're
not applying yet, having it ready changes everything.
Questions: parent — what would your kid put on a résumé today? kid — which 3 places would you
actually want to work, and why?

**Week 23 — The First Interview** · slug: `the-first-interview`
Big idea: Interviews aren't scary once you've practiced. A few prepared answers and basic manners
beat most other applicants.
Teach: common questions, simple strong answers, what to wear, eye contact/handshake, showing up
early, asking one good question; how nerves are normal.
Try this together: run a 10-minute mock interview at the kitchen table — you play the manager. Then
swap notes (kindly).
Questions: parent — what interview advice do you wish you'd had? kid — what question scares you
most, and how would you answer it?

**Week 24 — Be the Employee They Brag About** · slug: `be-the-employee-they-brag-about`
Big idea: Good jobs go to people with great references — and great references come from being the
worker a boss can't wait to recommend.
Teach: work ethic on the job (initiative, attitude, reliability); how bosses talk about you when
you're not there; the difference between bare-minimum and brag-worthy; why the first job's real
payoff is the reference.
Try this together: if your kid has a job, name 2 ways to go beyond the minimum next shift. If not,
apply the same to school/team.
Questions: parent — would a past boss brag about you? why? kid — what would make your boss/teacher
recommend you without hesitating?

**Week 25 — References Are Currency** · slug: `references-are-currency`
Big idea: The relationships your kid builds at work and school become the keys to the next door.
Teach: how to build, keep, and ask for references; staying in touch; the long compounding value of
a good name across years; leaving every job/role on good terms.
Try this together: help your kid identify 2–3 adults (boss, coach, teacher) who'd vouch for them,
and one way to strengthen each relationship this month.
Questions: parent — who are your references, and do you keep in touch? kid — who would vouch for
you right now?

**Week 26 — Quarter Review + Celebrate the Climb** · slug: `quarter-review-celebrate-the-climb`
Big idea: Recap the work/reputation quarter and celebrate progress — count victories, not missed
checkpoints.
Teach: recap Q2 (ownership, skills, interest, applying, value-first, reputation, jobs, references);
reinforce celebrating wins; make growth visible.
Try this together: Family Money/Success Night — each name 3 wins from the quarter; celebrate.
Questions: parent — what surprised you about your kid this quarter? kid — what are you most proud
of building?

## QUARTER 3 — GROWING MONEY & AVOIDING TRAPS (Weeks 27–39)

**Week 27 — Time Is Your Superpower** · slug: `time-is-your-superpower`
Big idea: For growing money, *when* you start matters far more than how much. A teen has the one
thing no millionaire can buy back — time.
Teach: the famous example — ~$20/mo for 3 years (~$720 total), left alone to age 60, can become
roughly $45,000 (with a lower-return caveat ~ $20,000); it's time, not the money, that does it;
starting at 30 means working far harder to catch up. Include the not-financial-advice caveat.
Try this together: run the numbers with your kid on a free compound-interest calculator; let the
final figure land.
Questions: parent — what would starting 10 years earlier have done for you? kid — what does seeing
that number make you want to do?

**Week 28 — Meet Compound Interest (Hands-On)** · slug: `meet-compound-interest-hands-on`
Big idea: Make compounding tangible so it stops being a vocabulary word and becomes a gut feeling.
Teach: how compound interest works in plain terms (money earns money, then that money earns money);
the rule-of-72 as a fun shortcut; small contributions + long time > big contributions + short time.
Caveat: illustrative, not advice.
Try this together: play with a calculator — change the start age, the monthly amount, the years —
and watch what moves the final number most (spoiler: time).
Questions: parent — which lever surprised you most? kid — what's more powerful, adding $50/mo or
starting 5 years sooner?

**Week 29 — The Custodial Account Conversation** · slug: `the-custodial-account-conversation`
Big idea: A teen can't open an investing account alone — they need a parent. So this is literally
where investing begins, and it's on you.
Teach: what a custodial/teen investing account is; how to open one (brokerages, basics); the
parent's role; starting tiny ($20) to build the habit. Practical and reassuring. Not advice.
Try this together: research one custodial/teen brokerage option this week; if you're ready, start
the setup together.
Questions: parent — what's stopping you from starting (and is it real)? kid — would you put $20 in
if I matched it?

**Week 30 — Boring Wins: Index Funds 101** · slug: `boring-wins-index-funds-101`
Big idea: At a teen's age, the winning move is almost embarrassingly boring — and trying to get
fancy is how people lose.
Teach: what an index fund is in plain terms (a tiny slice of hundreds of big companies at once,
e.g. an S&P 500 fund); why boring + steady + early beats clever + late; resist hot stocks/hype.
Caveat: illustrative, not advice; do your own research / consult a pro.
Try this together: look up what an S&P 500 index fund is together; discuss why "owning a little of
everything" is less risky than betting on one company.
Questions: parent — do you understand what you're invested in? kid — why might "boring" be smart
here?

**Week 31 — The 401(k) and "Free Money"** · slug: `the-401k-and-free-money`
Big idea: When your kid one day has a real job, an employer match is free money — and skipping it
is leaving a raise on the table.
Teach: what a 401(k) is, simply; what "matching" means and why it's free money; "always grab the
match"; planting this seed now so it's automatic later. Keep it future-facing and simple.
Try this together: if you have a 401(k), show your kid your match (or the concept) — make the
abstract real with your own example.
Questions: parent — are you grabbing your full match? kid — if your future boss offers to add 50¢
for every $1 you save, what should you do?

**Week 32 — The Trap That Looks Like Help** · slug: `the-trap-that-looks-like-help`
Big idea: The most dangerous money traps don't look like traps — they look like favors. Debt is the
favor that bills you for years.
Teach: "buy now, pay later," easy credit, "you deserve it" — kindness on the outside, trap inside;
how small, harmless-feeling charges compound into a chain; teaching a kid to see the costume.
Try this together: spot 3 "favor that's actually a trap" offers in the wild this week (ads, store
checkouts, apps) and decode them together.
Questions: parent — what "favor" cost you later? kid — what's an offer that's designed to feel like
a gift but isn't?

**Week 33 — How a Credit Card Actually Tricks You** · slug: `how-a-credit-card-tricks-you`
Big idea: The trick is the tiny minimum payment that makes an expensive thing look affordable.
Teach: how interest works on a balance; the minimum-payment illusion; "$100 swiped can cost ~$130";
"would you pay 30% extra at the register for no reason?"; carrying a balance vs. paying in full;
the one rule — a card is not for buying what you don't have the money for.
Try this together: do the math on a real example — a $500 purchase paid only at the minimum — and
see the true cost and time. (Free calculators exist.)
Questions: parent — have you ever carried a balance, and what did it cost? kid — would you pay
extra for a TV just because? then why do it with a card?

**Week 34 — "Buy Now, Pay Later" Is the Same Trap** · slug: `buy-now-pay-later-same-trap`
Big idea: BNPL is the same debt trap in a slicker app. "Four easy payments" is a lie about what
"afford" means.
Teach: how BNPL works and why it's engineered to feel painless; how it normalizes debt for a
generation; spotting it at checkout; the simple default — don't.
Try this together: next time your kid wants something, try the "could you pay the whole thing right
now?" test out loud — and wait 48 hours before buying either way.
Questions: parent — has BNPL ever crept into your spending? kid — why do you think these apps make
it SO easy to split the payment?

**Week 35 — What "Affordable" Really Means** · slug: `what-affordable-really-means`
Big idea: Redefine the most misused word in money. Affordable isn't "can I make the payments" —
it's "can I pay in full right now and still meet my needs and goals."
Teach: the payment-trap definition vs. the real definition; how this one sentence dodges a thousand
traps; applying it to phones, cars, clothes; "if the answer's no, you can't afford it — yet."
Try this together: apply the real definition to one thing your kid wants right now; talk through it
without judgment.
Questions: parent — what have you "afforded" that you couldn't really? kid — by the real
definition, what can you actually afford today?

**Week 36 — Good Debt, Bad Debt, and the Exit Plan** · slug: `good-debt-bad-debt-exit-plan`
Big idea: Debt isn't always failure — but if you take it on, plan your way out as fast as humanly
possible.
Teach: a balanced view (a car to get to work, a home, sometimes school can be reasonable); the
real danger is debt with no exit plan; "you can plan INTO debt in 5 minutes — spend more planning
your way OUT"; not shaming anyone who has debt.
Try this together: discuss a hypothetical — your kid needs a $3,000 car. What's the smartest way in,
and the fastest way out? Sketch it.
Questions: parent — do your current debts have exit plans? kid — what would make a debt "worth it"
vs. a trap?

**Week 37 — Student Loans Without the Panic** · slug: `student-loans-without-the-panic`
Big idea: Demystify the biggest debt most kids will face — with a calm, practical principle, not
fear.
Teach: what student loans are, simply; the principle "if you work at all during school, chip away
at loans now — even a little — don't wait for graduation"; weighing college cost vs. payoff; trades
and alternatives as legitimate; borrowing only what's needed.
Try this together: if college is on the horizon, look up the real cost of one school your kid likes
vs. the likely starting salary of the field — talk it through.
Questions: parent — what do you wish you'd known about loans? kid — what's the plan to keep
borrowing as small as possible?

**Week 38 — Credit Scores, Explained Simply** · slug: `credit-scores-explained-simply`
Big idea: A credit score is a reputation for money — built the same way: small, consistent, honest
behavior over time.
Teach: what a credit score is and why it matters (apartments, loans, rates); what builds it (paying
on time, not maxing out) and what hurts it; how a young adult starts building it responsibly; the
reputation parallel (callback to Week 20).
Try this together: explain how your own credit affects real things (a car rate, a mortgage); if age-
appropriate, discuss how they'll build theirs at 18.
Questions: parent — do you know what's on your credit report? kid — how is a credit score like a
reputation?

**Week 39 — Quarter Review + Money That Grows** · slug: `quarter-review-money-that-grows`
Big idea: Recap the growing/protecting quarter and celebrate progress.
Teach: recap Q3 (time, compounding, custodial account, index funds, the match, debt traps, credit);
celebrate wins; check the habits from Q1 are still alive.
Try this together: Family Money Night — review the quarter; if you opened/funded an account, look
at it together; name 3 wins.
Questions: parent — what money move did you finally make? kid — what trap can you now spot a mile
away?

## QUARTER 4 — CHARACTER, CHOICES & THE ROAD AHEAD (Weeks 40–52)

**Week 40 — Want Less, Win More** · slug: `want-less-win-more`
Big idea: The less you need, the more you win. Contentment is a quiet superpower — and the freest
person in the room is the one who needs the least.
Teach: the "want more → be happy" treadmill that never arrives; how wanting less widens the gap and
buys options/freedom; "enough" is a feeling you choose, not an amount you reach.
Try this together: each name 3 things you already have that you're genuinely grateful for; talk
about the last purchase that made you happy a week later (most don't).
Questions: parent — what do you have that "past you" dreamed of? kid — what's something you wanted
badly that you barely think about now?

**Week 41 — Lifestyle Creep** · slug: `lifestyle-creep`
Big idea: A raise is not permission to inflate your life. Spend every raise and you stay just as
broke — only with nicer stuff.
Teach: how income rising + spending rising = running in place; keeping spending steady as income
grows is where wealth actually happens; teaching a kid this before they ever get a raise.
Try this together: talk about a time the family got more money — did life inflate to match? What
would "keep it steady" have bought instead?
Questions: parent — where has lifestyle crept up on you? kid — if your pay doubled, what would you
do differently than most people?

**Week 42 — The 30% Breathing Room Rule** · slug: `the-30-percent-breathing-room-rule`
Big idea: A simple target — keep at least 30% of take-home from going to "stuff" — and financial
stress drops dramatically. Not because you're rich, because you're free.
Teach: the breathing-room concept; why a gap of even modest size kills stress; how to aim for it;
freedom = being able to buy the bigger thing and choosing not to.
Try this together: estimate your kid's current "kept vs. spent" ratio and set a stretch target;
do the same, honestly, for yourself.
Questions: parent — what % of your take-home do you keep? kid — what would 30% saved feel like vs.
spending it all?

**Week 43 — The Highlight Reel** · slug: `the-highlight-reel`
Big idea: Social media is a highlight reel of people performing "more." Never compare your kid's
real life to someone else's performance.
Teach: how the feed manufactures inadequacy; the kid with the new phone "didn't progress, just got
something newer"; comparison as the thief of contentment; arming a teen with this lens.
Try this together: scroll a feed together for 5 minutes and name what's being performed/sold in
each post; flip it — what real life is hidden behind the highlight?
Questions: parent — what does social media make you feel you "need"? kid — whose feed makes you feel
behind, and is it even real?

**Week 44 — What Are You Really Buying?** · slug: `what-are-you-really-buying`
Big idea: A lot of spending isn't about the thing — it's about belonging, status, or a feeling. Name
the real purchase.
Teach: spending to fit in / impress / soothe; how status purchases never fill the hole; teaching a
kid to ask "what am I actually trying to buy here?"; real belonging never sends a bill later.
Try this together: pick one recent want and dig underneath it together — is it the item, or a
feeling/status? No judgment, just honesty.
Questions: parent — what have you bought to impress people who didn't matter? kid — what's a
purchase that was really about fitting in?

**Week 45 — Borrow Other People's Hindsight** · slug: `borrow-other-peoples-hindsight`
Big idea: There are two ways to learn — pay full price by living every mistake, or borrow the
lesson from someone who already paid. Borrowing is the best trade there is.
Teach: hindsight is transferable; mentors, grandparents, people-already-living-it; asking for
advice isn't weakness (esp. hard for teens, who feel it's admitting they can't); this whole
membership is borrowed hindsight.
Try this together: help your kid identify one adult who's living something they admire, and draft
one question to ask them this month.
Questions: parent — whose hindsight saved you? kid — who could you learn from instead of figuring
it all out alone?

**Week 46 — How to Spot Good Advice From Bad** · slug: `how-to-spot-good-advice-from-bad`
Big idea: A simple filter — look at the life of the person giving the advice. Don't take directions
to a place from someone who's never been there.
Teach: teens get tons of advice, much of it bad; judge advice by the advisor's actual life/results;
be polite but careful with advice from people whose life you wouldn't want; finding trustworthy
sources.
Try this together: think of two people who give your kid advice (online or in life) — would you
want their actual life? Use that as the filter.
Questions: parent — whose advice do you take, and why them? kid — who do you listen to, and does
their life back it up?

**Week 47 — Choose Your Crowd** · slug: `choose-your-crowd`
Big idea: A kid becomes the average of the people closest to them — slowly, invisibly. Choosing the
crowd is choosing the future.
Teach: "show me your friends and I'll show you who you'll become"; friends mold us whether we notice
or not; steering toward people who are building/growing; how parents can influence the crowd without
forbidding (the hard part).
Try this together: talk (warmly, not as an interrogation) about who lifts your kid up vs. drags them
down; name one relationship worth investing more in.
Questions: parent — who are you becoming the average of? kid — which friends make you better, and
how?

**Week 48 — The Few Expensive Mistakes** · slug: `the-few-expensive-mistakes`
Big idea: Most mistakes are cheap and good for you. A small handful are expensive — they bill you
for years. Help your kid see those few coming.
Teach: cheap mistakes (try, fail, learn) vs. expensive ones (the few life-altering choices); the
parent's job isn't to prevent all mistakes but to flag the expensive aisle; let kids fail cheaply
and often. (Keep it universal — peer pressure, big impulsive money moves, choices with lasting
consequences. NO addiction content.)
Try this together: distinguish, with your kid, between a "cheap mistake" (bomb a test, buy a dud)
and an "expensive" one (something hard to undo); agree the cheap ones are allowed.
Questions: parent — what cheap mistakes taught you the most? kid — what's a mistake that would be
worth avoiding vs. one that's fine to make?

**Week 49 — Taming the Need to Fit In** · slug: `taming-the-need-to-fit-in`
Big idea: The hunger to belong is human and good — but the moment it drives the car, it steers a
kid into expensive choices. Tame it and you tame most of the trouble.
Teach: belonging is a real need, not a flaw; the danger is letting it make decisions; helping a kid
find belonging in the right places (people who accept the real them); the link between fitting-in
and overspending/peer pressure.
Try this together: talk about where your kid feels they truly belong as themselves vs. where they
perform; name one "real belonging" place to invest in.
Questions: parent — where do you still feel pressure to fit in? kid — where can you be fully
yourself, and where do you feel you have to perform?

**Week 50 — The Gift of Giving** · slug: `the-gift-of-giving`
Big idea: Generosity is a money skill and a happiness skill. Teaching a kid to give builds
gratitude, perspective, and a healthier relationship with money.
Teach: giving as an antidote to the want-more treadmill; how generosity builds character and
contentment; budgeting a "give" bucket; modeling it; small acts count. (Universal, not religious —
giving to causes, people, community.)
Try this together: add a small "give" bucket to your kid's money plan; pick one cause or person to
help this month, together.
Questions: parent — when has giving made you feel richer, not poorer? kid — what cause or person
would you want to help, and how?

**Week 51 — Your Kid's First Five-Year Plan** · slug: `your-kids-first-five-year-plan`
Big idea: Turn the whole year into something a kid can act on — a simple plan with big dreams at the
top and tiny first steps at the bottom. A goal without a plan is just a daydream.
Teach: dream big at the top, but the work is always the small daily steps; the simple starter plan
(a job/hustle; start investing small; a rough after-high-school direction; small steps now); don't
plan alone — borrow hindsight; count victories, not checkpoints; revisit it regularly. Include a
one-page plan template the family can fill in.
Try this together: fill out a one-page five-year plan WITH your kid (dream / direction / this
year's goals / tiny first steps / who they'll ask for help / how they'll celebrate wins).
Questions: parent — what's your own five-year plan (model it)? kid — what's the big dream, and what's
the very first tiny step?

**Week 52 — A Letter to Future You** · slug: `a-letter-to-future-you`
Big idea: Capstone. Bring the year full circle — back to Future You from Week 1 — and celebrate how
far the family has come. Stack your victories.
Teach: recap the year's arc (mindset → money basics → work/reputation → growing money/avoiding
traps → character/planning); the through-line of compounding and small consistent choices; this
isn't the end, it's a foundation; celebrate everything.
Try this together: each write a short letter to "Future You" (revisit the Week 1 paragraphs) —
what you've learned this year and the promise you're making forward. Seal them, set a date to open.
Questions: parent — how has the way you talk about money/success with your kid changed this year?
kid — what's the most important thing you'll carry into "future you's" life?
